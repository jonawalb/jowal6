A promise to defend an ally is only as good as the challenger’s belief that it will be kept. Stack commitment devices and watch credibility, deterrence and the patron’s costs move. Every model number is notional; the commitments listed below the model are real and sourced.

How to read it

Three players: a patron that has promised protection, an ally, and a challenger deciding whether to attack the ally. The patron knows how much the ally is worth to it; the challenger does not. Deterrence works when the challenger believes the patron would fight. That belief is credibility.

The problem is sharpest when the patron would pay dearly for the war, as when the challenger can strike the patron’s own cities. Would Washington trade Boston for Bonn? Commitment devices change the answer in two ways. Some tie hands: breaking them is costly, so the patron is likelier to fight. Some sink costs: they are paid in peacetime, and can show the challenger how much the patron cares. A forward force also works as a tripwire: an attack engages the patron whether it wants a war or not.

Method · The model

The patron’s value of defending the ally is \(v\), private. The challenger believes \(v\sim U[L,1]\), with \(L=0\) unless screening is on. Fighting costs the patron \(w\). A package of devices sums to a cost of backing out \(A=\sum a_j\), a peacetime cost \(S=\sum s_j\), a denial term \(D=\sum d_j\), and a chance of automatic defense \(T=1-\prod(1-t_j)\).

Patron. If the ally is attacked, the patron is drawn in automatically with probability \(T\); otherwise it fights iff \(v-w+A\ge0\). Credibility is

$$\kappa=1-(1-T)\,\Pr(v<w-A).$$

Challenger. Its gain from an unopposed attack is \(x\sim U[0,1]\); if the patron fights it loses \(K=k_C+D\). It attacks iff \((1-\kappa)\,x>\kappa K\), so

$$\Pr(\text{deterred})=\min\Big\{1,\ \frac{\kappa K}{1-\kappa}\Big\}.$$

Method · Readouts

\(\Pr(\text{war})=\Pr(\text{attack})\,\kappa\) and \(\Pr(\text{abandoned})=\Pr(\text{attack})(1-\kappa)\). Entrapment, given an attack, is the chance the patron fights although \(v<w\): \(\Pr(v<w)-(1-T)\Pr(v<w-A)\). The expected audience cost is \(\Pr(\text{attack})(1-T)\Pr(v<w-A)\,A\), paid only when a tied-hands patron backs out anyway.

Screening. With screening on, the challenger infers from a peacetime cost \(S\) that \(v\ge S\) (Fearon’s sinking-costs logic: a patron that valued the ally less would not pay). This is an assumption about the challenger’s inference, not a derived equilibrium.

Waterfall order. Devices are added in the order listed, so each bar is the marginal gain given the ones above it. Because automaticity multiplies, a different order can split the total differently.

Method · What is notional, and the evidence

Every parameter value is notional: the device costs \(a,s,t,d\), \(w\), \(k_C\) and the uniform beliefs. They are set to show the mechanisms, not to describe any real alliance. Change them under “Device parameters.”

The mechanisms come from the literature. Schelling treats forces stationed on allied territory as a commitment that makes involvement close to automatic (the \(t\) of the tripwire). Fearon separates tying hands (\(a\)) from sinking costs (\(s\)). Huth (1988) finds that a defender able to deny the attacker a quick and decisive victory deters better, which the denial term \(d\) stands for. Huth and Russett (1984) find that success is not systematically associated with a formal alliance or with nuclear weapons, but is associated with close economic and political ties and a favorable local balance. Fuhrmann and Sechser (2014) find that formal alliances with nuclear states deter, but that stationing nuclear weapons on a protégé’s territory does not bolster the effect. The model’s nuclear-sharing automaticity is therefore small by default; set it to zero to match their finding.

Snyder (1984) frames the patron’s dilemma as abandonment against entrapment; the frontier chart shows that trade.
