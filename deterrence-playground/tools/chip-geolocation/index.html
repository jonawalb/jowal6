Verifier's policy

How to read it

Each landmark server times a signed challenge to the chip. The round-trip time, multiplied by the speed the verifier assumes and halved, is the farthest the chip can be from that landmark: a circle on the map. The chip must sit where all circles overlap. The verdict asks one question: does any part of that region touch the country you want to rule out?

The Verifier view starts from an honest chip. Add landmarks near it and the region shrinks; slow the network and it grows. The Attacker view puts a smuggled chip inside the country to rule out and gives you the tricks from the literature. Delay and relays only grow the region. You can fool the verifier only by beating its speed limit, which a sound verifier never lets you do. Ambiguity is the attacker's real weapon: near a border, with few landmarks or a noisy network, the verifier cannot tell an honest chip from a smuggled one.

Method

Round trip. For a landmark at straight-line distance d from the chip: RTT = 2 × (d × (1 + route inflation) ÷ 200 km/ms + equipment latency) + jitter + any added delay. This follows CSET's Appendix B model without its local-loop term. A dark-fiber link has no route inflation and 0.1 ms equipment latency (CSET's best case); a radio link travels at 99.7 percent of c. With a proxy, the landmark reaches the proxy over the ordinary internet and the proxy forwards to the chip over the chip's link. The chip's own signing time is assumed known and subtracted, as in CSET.

Bound. dmax = v × RTT ÷ 2, with v set by the verifier: 299.79 km/ms (light in vacuum), 200 km/ms (light in fiber), or 200 ÷ (1 + route inflation) km/ms (calibrated). The first never fails. The second fails only against links faster than fiber. The third fails against any link straighter than the internet average.

Region and verdict. The tool samples a latitude-longitude grid inside the smallest circle, keeps points inside every circle, and weights them by area. It also samples every circle's edge about every 3 km and keeps the points inside all other circles, which traces the region's whole boundary. Countries come from a raster of Natural Earth 1:50m borders at 0.125° (about 14 km). To keep "ruled out" conservative, each sample also checks its neighbours one raster cell away, so a region within about 14 km of the border counts as touching it; "proven inside" requires every sample and its neighbours to be inside. A piece of the country smaller than the grid and wholly inside the region, away from its edge, could still be missed, so the verdict says "no sampled part".

Which country. By default the country to rule out is China together with Hong Kong and Macau. Since December 23, 2020, the EAR no longer treats Hong Kong as a separate destination from China (85 FR 83765). Macau remains a separate destination, but recent advanced-computing rules name it alongside Country Group D:5 (for example, RIN 0694-AK50). Any single country on the map can be chosen instead.

What is notional: route inflation, equipment latency, jitter, attacker delays and link speeds, landmark placement, and the pass policies. Defaults sit inside CSET's best-to-worst range (0.1 to 2 ms one-way equipment latency, 0 to 20 percent route inflation). Landmarks are placed at city centres from Natural Earth; no real landmark sites, data centres or facilities are shown. Left out: repeated probes and minimum filtering, landmark takeover, key extraction or attestation attacks, chips in transit, and the cost of running landmarks.
