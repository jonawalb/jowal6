    The submarine's rules

    Changing a rule restarts the hunt. The seed fixes the sub's path and every die, so the same link replays the same hunt.

    Run 200 hunts

    A simple automatic hunter plays 50 hunts at each of four sprint rates against the behaviour you picked. It sends the ship to the likeliest cell, lays buoys every third hour and an aircraft every sixth, and attacks once its map puts 50% inside the ring (or on the last hour).

    How to play

    The goal. A submarine is heading south from the Norwegian Sea, or loitering, and you do not know where. An opening cue puts it inside the dashed ring. You have 36 hours and 16 search points to find it and one attack to make.

    Each hour. Pick a tool and click the map. A sonobuoy field listens for 8 hours inside its circle. An aircraft sweep searches a square box for 2 hours, starting the hour after you call it. The towed-array ship is free but slow: click where it should go. Then advance the clock. You can undo anything you did this hour.

    The haze. The shaded map is your belief about where the sub is. It spreads as time passes because the sub moves. When a sensor searches an area and hears nothing, probability drains out of that area and piles up elsewhere. A contact pulls probability toward it, but some contacts are whales, wrecks and noise. Clues arrive at hours 6, 15 and 24, each with a stated reliability.

    The decision. Prosecute when you think the ring is on the sub. The panel shows how much of your map sits inside the ring before you commit. If the sub reaches an exit point it has broken out, and you lose.

    Afterwards. The reveal draws the true track over your search, marks which contacts were real, and charts how much of your map's probability sat near the sub each hour. The link in the address bar replays your exact hunt.

    Method and notional values

    Detection follows classical search theory. The sonobuoy field and aircraft use Koopman's random search formula, P = 1 − e−WL/A, where W is the sweep width, L the length of search track and A the area (Koopman 1946, p. 28). For the buoy field, which sits still, the sub's own speed supplies the track length. The towed array uses a lateral range curve, the chance of detection against how close the sub passes, whose area is the sweep width (Koopman, p. 24).

    The haze is a particle filter: 4,000 possible submarines, each moved by the same rules as the real one, each reweighted every hour by Bayes' rule given what your sensors did and did not hear. That is how the AF 447 search map was built (Stone et al. 2014), and the approach traces back to the Scorpion search in 1968. The opening ring is a version of the "flaming datum" problem: the longer you wait, the larger the area the sub could be in (Washburn 1980).

    Your map uses the true rules and the true sprint rate. Real searchers never have that. The only unknowns here are the dice, the route and, if you pick "Unknown", the behaviour. Treat your score as an upper bound.

    The geography is Natural Earth coastlines. Everything tactical is notional: the sensor fields, ranges and sweep widths, the aircraft and ship performance, the sub's speeds and routes, the start area, false-contact rates, clue reliabilities and the attack radius. None describes a real system, patrol area or operation. The table lists every value.

    Left out: sound propagation, thermal layers and weather; ice; the sub's own sonar and weapons; real surveillance networks; allied and opposing forces; and the time needed to prosecute. The game teaches how search reasoning works, not how any real hunt would go.
