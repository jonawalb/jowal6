Surfaces and Gaps

Marine Corps doctrine tells its forces to avoid enemy strengths, which it calls surfaces, and to focus on enemy weaknesses, which it calls gaps. Gaps have to be sought out, they rarely last, and the main effort may shift to the unit that found one. This bench model shows that search and that shift.

This section follows the view you are on. Switch views with the buttons under the card or the V key; the same model and readout drive both. Reset puts the figure back where it started.

How to read it: the machine

What is on the bench

  1. The wall face on the left has five lanes. In each lane a key either sits flush with the face (a surface, a hard spot) or is set back into the wall (a gap, a soft spot).
  2. The feeler blades sit on five carriages, each pushed by its own spring from one bar.
  3. The lit ram rides a saddle on the bar. It stands for the main effort.

How it moves

  1. The bar. Push the bar and every blade moves forward together. A blade that meets a surface stops and its spring compresses; a blade over a gap slips in until it reaches its full stroke.
  2. The ram. Choose the lane it is planned to strike with the pointer, the slider or the arrow keys, then press Probe the face.
  3. If the planned lane is a surface, the ram strikes it and stops. It then withdraws, slides across to the lane whose blade went deepest, and drives through that gap.
  4. Shift the face resets the keys. In the idle tour the face shifts after every probe, so the gap the ram just used is usually closed the next time round.

How to read it: the battlefield

The scale is a rifle platoon; blue is friendly and red is the enemy.

What is on the ground

  1. The enemy. Across the top, the enemy holds five lanes, each with a rifle pair (in the centre lane a machine-gun team, whose sector is the red fan on the ground). Each enemy element sits either in its forward fighting position, a surface, or back in a position in depth, which leaves the ground in front of it unwatched: a gap. The farther a bench key is set back, the farther back that lane’s element sits. Wire runs between the lanes.
  2. The probing teams. From the line of departure, the chalk line on pegs, five fire teams probe the five lanes together, each built around a team leader, an automatic rifleman, a grenadier and a rifleman (ATP 3-21.8, para. 1-11) and moving in the wedge, “the basic formation of the fire team” (para. 3-46).
  3. The main effort. The lit main effort is the planned lane’s team and the platoon’s follow-on element behind it: a fire team with its squad leader, the platoon leader and his radio operator.

How it moves

  1. The controls. Choose the lane planned for the main effort with the pointer, the Planned lane slider or the arrow keys, then press Probe the face, click or press Enter to send the probe. Shift the face, or S, lets the enemy reposition.
  2. The probe. In a movement to contact, the platoon manual says, the lead element that meets a stationary enemy “tries to identify any crew-served weapon or antitank (AT) weapon positions, the enemy’s flanks, and gaps in positions” (para. 4-67). A team that reaches a surface goes to ground under fire and trades fire with the position; a team in a gap slips in.
  3. The shift. If the planned lane is a surface, the follow-on element strikes it and is stopped, falls back, moves across behind the line and follows the team that went deepest through its gap; the main effort shifts to that team, and once through it fires into the flanks of the positions beside the gap. The teams held on surfaces stay down and keep those positions busy.
  4. The enemy moves. After each probe the enemy elements run to new positions, so the gaps move.
  5. Losses. Success is not free: greyed figures lying still are men down on both sides. In the model, the platoon medic goes to the first blue casualty, and the platoon sergeant kneels at the casualty collection point behind the line of departure; the manual says the platoon sergeant traditionally establishes and operates that point, which includes “directing the platoon combat medic and aid/litter teams in moving casualties” (para. 1-22). The readout ends with the count, blue against red.

Soldiers are drawn larger than scale (about one and a half times) so they read; the enemy’s strength and positions, sizes, ranges, timings and the casualty counts are notional.

What doctrine says

The Marine Corps manual MCDP 1, Warfighting (1997) puts it simply: “surfaces are hard spots—enemy strengths—and gaps are soft spots—enemy weaknesses.” Marines “avoid enemy strength and focus our efforts against enemy weakness,” and “Whenever possible, we exploit existing gaps. Failing that, we create gaps” (p. 92). A gap need not be a hole in a line. It may be “any weakness in time, space, or capability,” such as “a boundary between two units” (pp. 92–93).

The manual adds that judging them takes care: “What is a surface in one case may be a gap in another,” and Marines should expect an enemy to disguise his dispositions “to lure us against a surface that appears to be a gap.” Gaps “will rarely be permanent and will usually be fleeting,” and the manual says forces must “actively seek out gaps by continuous and aggressive reconnaissance.” And “if our main effort has struck a surface but another unit has located a gap, we designate the second unit as the main effort and redirect our combat power in support of it” (p. 93).

The bench shows only the simplest case: gaps that already exist, in space, found by probing. It does not create gaps, and its blades cannot be fooled by a disguised surface.

What is notional

Every number on the bench is a teaching value, not data. A key set back 6 mm or more counts as a gap; less is a surface. A blade can reach 16 mm past the face and the ram 24 mm, so the readout reports the blade’s depth up to 16 mm and the ram’s up to 24 mm. The four faces the tour steps through are invented: each has one deep gap, and one face also has a shallow 9 mm gap that loses to the deep one. The rule that the ram goes to the deepest blade is the bench’s stand-in for “the unit that located a gap”; in this model a deeper probe means a better gap, which real gaps need not follow.

The casualty counts in the readout and the Battlefield view are notional too, set so that success is never free. Once a probe makes contact, blue loses one man for every two teams stopped on a key (rounded up) and red one for every two (rounded down); every face in the tour stops four teams, so 2 against 2. If the main effort first strikes a surface, blue loses two more; when it goes through the gap, red loses one more. Planning the main effort on the gap therefore ends 2 against 3, and planning it on a surface 4 against 3. The counts start again with each probe. These are teaching values, not loss rates from data or doctrine.

Source: U.S. Marine Corps, MCDP 1, Warfighting, 20 June 1997, pp. 92–93 (archived copy); Headquarters, Department of the Army, ATP 3-21.8, Infantry Rifle Platoon and Squad (archived copy of the official PDF), 11 January 2024, incorporating Change 1 (June 2025), paras. 1-11, 1-22, 3-46 and 4-67.
