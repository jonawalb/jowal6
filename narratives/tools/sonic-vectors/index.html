In Walberg’s model a sender has two ways to move belief. It can fake the evidence, or it can leave the evidence true and use a song to change how much weight listeners give it. This tool asks when the second way wins.

Notional model

Question: Can cheap emotional audio move belief more efficiently than faking evidence, and why do text fact-checks miss it?

What you do: Make music cheaper, time a correction, or let one melody reach everyone.

What you will see: The sender’s best mix of fake evidence and music, belief paths after a fact-check, and how many listeners act together.

Limits: A theory model with notional parameters and no data. The experiment in the research design has not been run.

Presenting this in 3 minutes
  1. When music is costly the sender fakes evidence (f = 1.85) and buys no music. Make music cheap and it buys full arousal (A = 1) and cuts the fake to f = 0.80. That is P1, substitution.
  2. Both attacks start at the same belief. A text fact-check at hour 6 removes 72% of the fake’s excess belief and none of the song’s, because the song’s evidence is true. The song’s excess falls to the fake’s corrected level only at hour 21, as the feeling fades. A correction that names the feeling reverses the pattern. That is P2.
  3. On average 34% of listeners act either way. With one melody the share swings from episode to episode: in 29 of 300 episodes more than half act at once; with independent moods, in none. That is P3, coordination.
  4. Click through M1 to M5 and watch which term of the formula each one changes. M5 puts the claim in the lyric: ordinary propaganda with sound. M1 to M4 are the new part.

Takeaway: In this model a song changes the weight people give true evidence, so it can stand in for faking evidence, it slips past fact-checks that only test the evidence, and one melody moves many people at once.

Say aloud: This is a theory model with notional numbers. Nothing here is measured, and the survey experiment the design proposes has not been run.

P1 · Does cheap music replace faked evidence? notional

f: how much stronger the fake makes the evidence look, on a log scale (f = 0.7 roughly doubles it). A: how much the song arouses listeners, 0 to 1. The sender picks both to maximise the belief it produces minus what it spends. Arousal raises the weight on evidence: λ = 1 + κ · A · congruence. Costs and the sender’s value of belief share one notional unit: moving belief from 0% to 100% is worth 1.

P2 · Why does a text fact-check miss the song? notional

Faked evidence, no musicTrue evidence plus a songBelief the true evidence justifies

Both attacks are sized to produce the same belief at hour 0. A text fact-check removes a share of the faked evidence (it acts on s and L). Naming the feeling and its source removes a share of the arousal (it acts on a). By default the feeling halves every 12 hours; the fake does not fade. That the fact-check cannot touch the song is the proposition’s premise built into the model, not a test of it. The default 70% correction effect is notional and generous: Lewandowsky et al. (2012) review evidence that corrections often leave belief in place. The point is which term each correction targets, not that fact-checks work well.

P3 · Does one melody move a crowd together? notional

Independent moodsOne melody (current setting)

300 listeners with different priors hear the same true evidence in each of 300 episodes. Each episode brings a new song and new moods. Every listener’s arousal has the same spread in both settings; only how much of it is shared changes.

Five ways a song can act on belief

posterior ∝ π · L(s|c)λ(a)

π: prior belief that the claim is true. s: the evidence. L(s|c): how much more likely that evidence is if the claim is true. a: the listener’s feeling (arousal, valence). λ(a): the weight the feeling puts on the evidence; 1 is an ordinary Bayesian.

Method · the model

Walberg’s model gives a receiver a prior π that a claim is true and evidence s with likelihood ratio L(s|c). An ordinary Bayesian multiplies prior odds by L. The affect-modulated receiver raises L to a power set by its feeling a = (arousal, valence): posterior ∝ π · L(s|c)λ(a). In log-odds, logit(posterior) = logit(π) + λ(a) · ℓ, with ℓ = log L. λ rises with arousal and with how well the feeling’s valence matches the claim. This tool uses the simplest such form, λ = 1 + κ · arousal · congruence. The Attacking the Update tool works through the same weighting idea with bell-curve beliefs.

P1, substitution. The sender holds true but weak evidence (ℓ0). It can add fabricated evidence f at cost cs per unit and buy arousal A in [0, 1] with music at cost cm per unit. It chooses both to maximise τ · posterior − csf − cmA, by grid search. Because belief cannot pass 100%, a strong song leaves less for a fake to add, so the two are substitutes near the top: as cm falls, the sender buys more music and less fake. The larger κ (how much arousal raises the weight), the bigger the shift.

P2, asymmetric defense. Two attacks start at the same belief: faked evidence f = (λ(a0) − 1)ℓ0 with no music, and the true evidence with a song at arousal a0. Arousal halves every h hours, and the tool assumes the extra weight fades with it. A text fact-check removes a share e of the faked evidence. The song’s evidence is true, so a fact-check has nothing to remove there: this is the proposition’s premise built into the model, not a test of it. A correction that names the feeling removes a share e of the arousal and does nothing to the fake. The default e = 70%, applied at once and never wearing off, is notional and generous: Lewandowsky et al. (2012) review evidence that corrections often leave belief in place. The panel shows which term each correction targets, not how well fact-checks work.

P3, coordination. Each listener’s arousal is Φ(√w · zcommon + √(1 − w) · zi), which is uniform on [0, 1] for every w. So w changes only how much of the feeling is shared, not its average or spread, and the average share who act stays the same. What changes is how often many listeners cross the line at once. The design links this to cascade models of mass mobilization; this tool shows only the correlated updates, not a cascade.

The five mechanisms. The research design maps where a sonic attacker intervenes in the tuple (a, π, s, L, λ): M1 emotional priming acts on a; M2 mnemonic compression on a and the memory of s; M3 in-group signaling on π through identity; M4 narrative compression on L through framing; M5 a literal claim in the lyric on s. M5 is standard signal falsification with sonic delivery, ordinary propaganda with sound. M1 to M4 are the new part.

What is notional

    The design proposes a 2 × 2 × 2 survey experiment (song versus instrumental, two claim domains, true versus false claims) and a catalogue of song-event cases. Neither has been run or built. No number here comes from data.
