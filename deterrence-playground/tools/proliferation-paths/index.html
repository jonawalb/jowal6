Where the datasets disagree

B Bleek 2017 · W Singh & Way, Way's 2012 update · J Jo & Gartzke 2007 (codes pursuit and acquisition only). Each column gives the first year coded at that stage or higher, so a state that skips a stage shows its later start there. "No activity" is the first year back at no activity, put on one convention; "open" means still coded in the dataset's last year. Shaded cells differ by three years or more.

How to read it

Each dataset sorts nuclear behavior into stages. Exploring means leaders authorized study of a weapons option. Pursuing means concrete steps toward a weapon, such as a cabinet-level decision, weaponization work or dedicated technology. Acquired means the state tested or assembled a nuclear device. A state can skip stages, go back down, or stop altogether, which this tool calls reversal.

The three datasets use nearly the same categories but different evidence and different start years, so their dates differ, sometimes by a decade. Pick one dataset to read its story, or "Compare all three" to see the lanes side by side. The map then shades each state by the highest stage any dataset codes and hatches the ones where they disagree.

Only what the datasets code is shown. A blank means none of them codes that state in that year, not that nothing happened.

Method

  • Codings are parsed by scripts/build_data.py from the saved source files: Way's "Nuclear Proliferation Dates" (revised 12 June 2012), Table 1 of Bleek (2017, p. 8) and Table 1 of the Jo and Gartzke codebook (September 2006, p. 13). The only hand-transcribed material is the state-card notes and the original Singh and Way codings reported in Bleek's footnotes, each tied to a page, note or document.
  • Start years. Singh and Way begin in 1945, so they never code U.S., British or Soviet wartime activity, Germany or Japan. Bleek and Jo and Gartzke begin in 1939. The timeline hatches years a dataset does not cover.
  • End years. Way's file was last revised in 2012, Bleek published in June 2017, and Jo and Gartzke's codebook covers 1939 to 2002 (their replication file stops at 1992). An open-ended coding is drawn to the dataset's last year and labelled "open"; the slider stops at 2017 because no dataset goes further.
  • Two conventions for the last year. Bleek's second year is the year the state dropped back ("returned to no activity in 1991"); Way and Jo and Gartzke give the last active year. The tool draws Bleek's spans one year shorter so both mean the same thing.
  • Explore is Bleek's and Way's category only. Jo and Gartzke code a program (treated here as pursuit) and possession (acquisition). Bleek's table lists exploration as a running span beneath pursuit; Way's stages do not overlap. The tool shows the highest stage coded in each year, which makes the two layouts comparable.
  • Checks. Jo and Gartzke's codebook table matches their replication data for every state-year to 1992 except South Africa's possession in 1991 (in the table, not in the data). Way's 2011 and 2012 files differ in three places, listed below. Bleek's narrative dates Romanian exploration from 1965 while his Table 1 gives 1978; the tool draws the table and says so on Romania's card.
  • Singh and Way (2004) original. The article's own table is paywalled and was not obtained. Where Bleek's footnotes report the original codings, the state card shows them.
  • Mehta's reversal data. Rupal Mehta's work on nuclear reversal (Delaying Doomsday: The Politics of Nuclear Reversal, Oxford University Press, 2020) was requested for this tool, but no public data file from it could be found on her website or on Harvard Dataverse, and no work titled "Rolling Back the Bomb" turned up in OpenAlex. Reversals here are derived from the three datasets instead: a reversal is any year a dataset codes a state at a lower stage than the year before.
  • Map. Natural Earth 1:110m country outlines on today's borders. Ukraine, Belarus and Kazakhstan are shown because Soviet weapons remained on their territory after 1991; none of the three datasets codes them.
