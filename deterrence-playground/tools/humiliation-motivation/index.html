A public humiliation makes backing down costly at home. Mild humiliation is absorbed, moderate humiliation leaves only capable states challenging, and severe humiliation pushes weak states to challenge too. Move severity and legitimacy and watch what a challenge still tells the dominant power.

How to read it

The tool implements the formal model in Jonathan Walberg’s working paper Humiliation, Status, and Revisionist Motivation: A Formal Theory. A humiliated state H knows whether it is capable or weak; the dominant power D does not. H accommodates or challenges, and D concedes or resists a challenge. Humiliation enters through H’s domestic politics: the more severe the humiliation, and the less legitimate the order that imposed it, the more it costs a leader to back down.

  • The game tree shows every payoff at the current values. Heavy lines are the equilibrium path for each type.
  • The map colors each pair of severity and legitimacy by its equilibrium. The dashed lines are the paper’s thresholds s̲ and s̄.
  • The information curve plots Proposition 5: how much a challenge tells D, in bits, as severity rises.
  • The side panel names the equilibrium, explains your last change, and substitutes the current numbers into each proposition.
  • The address bar stores every parameter, so a link reproduces the exact setup.

Method · The game

Types and moves. Nature draws H’s type \(\theta\in\{\theta_H,\theta_L\}\) with \(\Pr(\theta_H)=\pi\). A humiliation of severity \(s\in[0,1]\) is public. H chooses accommodate \(A\) or challenge \(C\); after \(C\), D chooses concede \(K\) or resist \(R\).

H’s payoffs. Accommodating costs \(\alpha(s,\lambda)=\alpha_0\,s\,(1-\lambda)\), where \(\lambda\) is the perceived legitimacy of the status order. A conceded challenge pays \(v\). A resisted challenge pays \(p_i v-(1-p_i)\ell-k\), with \(p_H>p_L\).

D’s payoffs. \(0\) after \(A\); \(-d\) after conceding; \((1-\bar p)w-\bar p d-k_D\) after resisting, with \(\bar p(\mu)=\mu p_H+(1-\mu)p_L\). D concedes iff

$$\bar p(\mu)>p^*\equiv1-\frac{k_D}{w+d}.$$

Method · Propositions

1. Separating. The capable type challenges and the weak type accommodates iff \(p_Lv-(1-p_L)\ell-k+\alpha<0<p_Hv-(1-p_H)\ell-k+\alpha\) and \(p_H\le p^*\).

2. Status trap. With \(\bar p(\pi)\le p^*\), for \(s>\bar s\) both types challenge and D resists, where

$$\bar s=\frac{k+(1-p_L)\ell-p_Lv}{\alpha_0(1-\lambda)}.$$

3. Accommodation. For \(s<\underline s=\dfrac{k+(1-p_H)\ell-p_Hv}{\alpha_0(1-\lambda)}\), with beliefs that make D resist an unexpected challenge, both types accommodate.

Lemma 1 and Proposition 4. \(\bar s\) rises with \(k\), \(\ell\) and \(\lambda\) and falls with \(v\) and \(p_L\); as \(\lambda\to1\), \(\bar s\to\infty\).

5. Information. The information in a challenge is \(0\) below \(\underline s\), the prior entropy \(H(\pi)\) between \(\underline s\) and \(\bar s\), and \(0\) above \(\bar s\).

Method · What the tool adds and leaves out

To color the whole map, the tool checks all four pure strategies for H at each point. On the path of play D updates by Bayes’ rule; after a challenge nobody makes, D may hold any belief, so it may resist if \(p_L\le p^*\) and concede if \(p_H>p^*\). Where several equilibria exist, the map shows the first of separating, status trap, pooling with concession, accommodation; the panel lists all of them.

Where \(p_H>p^*\ge\bar p(\pi)\) and \(s\) lies between \(\underline s\) and \(\bar s\), no pure-strategy equilibrium exists. The paper does not characterize that case and the tool does not compute mixed strategies, so the map marks it gray. The tool does not apply refinements beyond this.

Sliders keep \(p_L<p_H\) and keep the weak type’s resisted-challenge payoff below zero, the case the paper analyzes (a weak state “likely to lose”). Legitimacy stops at 0.95 because \(\bar s\) grows without bound as \(\lambda\to1\). Every value is notional; case cards place each case at a notional point matching the paper’s verbal reading.
