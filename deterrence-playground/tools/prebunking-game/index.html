Most prebunking games teach you to spot tricks in a message. Here every report is honest and its strength is a number. The pull comes from outside the message, and the game measures how far it moves the weight you give the evidence.

Everything in the game is invented: the town, the reports, the comments and the headlines. It is a teaching and research prototype with no efficacy data.

Rounds

Loaded rounds and their neutral twins are mixed together. Which is which is shown after each part.

Sound

What the game reads

posterior odds = prior odds × LRλ

LR is the report's stated strength. The game solves for λ̂ from your answer. λ = 1 is the Bayesian weight. The form is from Walberg's working papers; the scoring built on it is notional.

How to read it

Each round asks a yes/no question about the invented town of Ardley. Two lines are evidence: a base rate ("about 30 in 100 calls like this turn out real") and a report with a stated strength ("2× as likely if it is real"). Bayes' rule turns those into one right answer. Everything else on the card, a comment thread, a drone, an earlier headline, a grievance, a countdown, carries no evidence.

From your answer the game infers λ̂, the weight you put on the report. At 1 you moved as far as the report justifies; above 1 further; between 0 and 1 less far; below 0 the wrong way. Each loaded round has a neutral twin with identical numbers, so the gap between the two is the part of your answer the feeling moved, not your arithmetic.

Part 2 adds three quick steps before each answer: place your feeling on a grid of pleasant to unpleasant and calm to activated, give it a name, and say what on the screen, or in you, produced it. Two of Part 2's pulls are new, to see whether the habit carries over.

How it differs from Bad News

This page used to hold a short game in the style of Bad News, the Cambridge and DROG game in which "players take on the role of a fake news producer and learn to master six documented techniques" (Roozenbeek and van der Linden 2019). Go Viral! and Harmony Square follow the same inoculation design. Borrowed Feelings departs from it on mechanism.

Why the scoring matters: a reanalysis of five studies found that Bad News and Go Viral! "did not improve discrimination, but rather elicited more 'false' responses to all news items (more conservative responding)" (Modirrousta-Galian and Higham 2023). Here every report is true and correctly stated, so blanket suspicion scores as badly as over-reaction.

What this is and isn't

It is a five-minute demonstration of an idea from Jonathan Walberg's working papers: that emotion changes how much weight people give evidence, and that a campaign can exploit this without faking anything. The λ form comes from the Sonic Vectors design and the Emotional Priors papers. The Emotional Priors paper predicts that teaching people "to notice when their reactions are being moved" should transfer across topics, while topic-specific hardening should not (its hypothesis H4). It also flags whether people who correctly attribute their arousal are still moved as the model's "most exposed seam." Part 2 is a toy version of both questions.

It isn't a validated measure or an intervention with evidence behind it. There are no efficacy data. Three twin pairs per part is far too few to say anything about a person; Part 2 always follows Part 1, so practice alone could shrink the gap; and the score mixes numeracy with feeling. In Walberg's simulation the transfer of affect-awareness follows from how awareness is specified, so it is a prediction, not a result.

What comes from published work

  • Feelings from one source can be misread as information about another. MacCormack and Lindquist (2019) found that hunger made people "experience negative emotions and to negatively judge a researcher, but only when participants are not aware that they are conceptualizing their affective state as emotions." The attribution step borrows this logic. Schwarz and Clore (1983) is the classic statement of mood as information.
  • Martel, Pennycook and Rand (2020): "inducing reliance on emotion resulted in greater belief in fake (but not real) news stories compared to a control or to inducing reliance on reason."
  • The feeling grid uses the two dimensions of affect, valence and arousal (Russell 1980). Barrett (2017): "Interoceptive sensations are usually experienced as lower dimensional feelings of affect." Feldman, Bliss-Moreau and Lindquist (2024) review how signals from the body give rise to valence and arousal.
  • Naming the feeling follows research on affect labeling, which "can attenuate our emotional experiences" (Torre and Lieberman 2018), and on emotion differentiation (Kashdan, Barrett and McKnight 2015).
  • The grievance round follows the fear and anger contrast in appraisal research (Lerner and Keltner 2001), which Walberg's model turns into two different operators. The crowd round draws on social baseline theory (Beckes and Coan 2011) and Walberg's working hypothesis that false stories persist while the group behind them does.

What is notional

  • The town, reports, base rates, report strengths, comments and headlines are invented.
  • λ̂ from one answer, answers clamped to 1–99%, λ̂ capped to −1…4, the distance |λ̂ − 1| capped at 3, the twin gap and the verdict thresholds (±0.15) are this game's choices.
  • The drone (two detuned low oscillators, a tritone, a slow pulse), the 15-second countdown and the share counter are design choices, not calibrated inductions.
