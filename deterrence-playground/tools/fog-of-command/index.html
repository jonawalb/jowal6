How to play

You command a Blue brigade holding the Varn valley. Red will attack down one of three roads, West, Center or East, and you must hold Tarn Crossing until 22:00. You cannot see Red. You only see reports, and they arrive late, miss things and are sometimes wrong. Each hour: task the drone to look at one sector, send orders (pick a unit, then click a sector), then press End hour. Orders take 0–2 hours to reach a unit. Your armored reserve can only be in one place.

      Scenario

      The number fixes Red's plan and every roll of the dice, so the same link replays the same game.

      Keys: 1–5 pick a unit, D the drone, Tab to a sector and Enter to send, N ends the hour.

      How it works

      The situation. Your brigade holds a line across the Varn valley: three mechanized battalions, one on each road, a reconnaissance squadron forward in Center Gap, and an armored battalion in reserve at Tarn Crossing. Red has nearly twice your strength. It will mass on one road (its main effort), make a noisy feint on another, probe the third, and later push a second echelon wherever it is winning. Which road is which changes with every scenario.

      Each hour. Task the drone to look at one sector. Send orders by picking a unit and clicking where it should go. An order takes 0, 1 or 2 hours to reach the unit, and then the unit moves at road speed: one hour per sector, two across the ridges or on the long roads to the crossing. Press End hour. Red moves, units in the same sector fight, and your sensors observe.

      Reports. Every sensor can miss what is there, name the wrong type, misjudge strength or report something that is not there. Red's decoys can pass as tank battalions. Each report carries the time it was seen and a confidence word from Sherman Kent's scale. A report can arrive an hour or more after the moment it describes.

      Winning. You hold if Tarn Crossing is still yours at 22:00. You lose the moment Red holds it with none of your units there.

      Afterwards. The review shows what you believed against what was true, hour by hour, and grades the confidence words. It then replays this scenario 1,000 times with your orders, with your plan re-aimed by perfect information, with your orders sent without delay, and with a scripted doctrinal commander, so you can see what the fog and the friction cost you.

      Method

      Fog and friction. Clausewitz wrote that three-fourths of what action in war depends on is "hidden more or less in the clouds of great uncertainty," that a "great part of the information obtained in War is contradictory, a still greater part is false," and that "everything is very simple in War, but the simplest thing is difficult." The game splits these apart: the report model is the fog; order delays and travel time are the friction. Boyd's OODA loop (observe, orient, decide, act) is the cycle you run each hour, and the delays are what make it slow.

      Combat. Units in the same sector fight hour by hour under a stochastic version of Lanchester's square law: each side's losses are proportional to the other side's fighting power. The defender's posture multiplies its fighting value by k. Under the square law a battle breaks even when the attacker's numbers exceed the defender's by √k, so k = 9 for a prepared defense and 6.25 for a hasty one reproduce the U.S. Army's historical minimum planning ratios of 3:1 and 2.5:1 (FM 5-0, Table B-1). A unit breaks at 50% losses, the same threshold for both sides, as in Mearsheimer's reconstruction of the 3:1 rule. Each unit also has a hidden quality factor, so ratios alone never settle a fight.

      Calibration. FM 5-0 says defenders have "over a 50 percent probability" of defeating an attacker about three times their strength. In 10,000 one-on-one trials of the game's combat model, a prepared defender at 3:1 holds 53.8% of the time; a hasty defender at 2.5:1 holds 53.3%; an even meeting engagement splits 50.9%.

      The critics. The 3:1 rule is a planning rule of thumb, and it is contested. Mearsheimer defends it for breakthrough battles only, in reply to Joshua Epstein, who offered historical evidence against it; Epstein answered in the same issue, and Dupuy disputed Mearsheimer's corrections to his combat data. Fits of Lanchester equations to the detailed daily records of Kursk and the Ardennes found that none of the basic laws fit well, and that which forces are actually engaged matters more than which law is used (Lucas and Turkes; Lucas and Dinges). Treat the combat here as a teaching model with a sourced anchor, not a prediction.

      The cost of fog. The review uses the idea behind the value of information (Howard 1966): run the same decision rule with and without perfect information on the same scenario, and the difference in outcomes is what the missing information was worth.

      Notional values. Everything else is notional: unit strengths, Red's plan and timings, movement times, the sensor detection and error rates, report delays, false-contact rates, order delays, the base kill rate and the noise. The table lists every value and why.

      Left out. Artillery and air as separate systems, supply, terrain effects beyond road times, night, civilians, friendly-unit reporting delays (your own positions are shown correctly), Red's own fog (Red reads the truth), and the chance that your staff counts one Red unit twice.
