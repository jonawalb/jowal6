      How to play

      You run the Anglo-American airlift from 26 June 1948, when the Soviet blockade closes the roads, railways and canals into West Berlin, until 12 May 1949, when it is lifted. Each turn is a day, and the buttons fly one day, one week or four weeks with your current plan. Dated events stop the clock and ask for a decision.

      Berlin needs a minimum tonnage every day: 4,500 tons until the end of 1948 and 5,620 from January. Food and coal come out of the city's reserves when you fall short. You lose if political support in Washington and London runs out, or if the food reserve stays empty for five days.

      Tonnage is aircraft times trips times load. Crews limit the trips each aircraft can fly, and the aircraft in commission cap them. Landing slots in Berlin then limit how many of those trips can land: one aircraft every three minutes per airfield in good weather, fewer in cloud and fog. The forecast table in the panel shows what your plan delivers tomorrow in each kind of weather and which limit binds.

      The chart compares your daily tonnage with the historical monthly averages from the USAFE summary and with single days the sources record, such as Air Force Day and the Easter Parade. After the season, drag the replay slider to step back through your airlift day by day.

      How the model works

      Trips. For each stream of aircraft, trips per day = min(aircraft × crews per aircraft × monthly hours ÷ 30 ÷ round-trip hours, aircraft in commission × 24 ÷ (round trip + ground time)). American C-54s fly from Rhein-Main and Wiesbaden down the southern corridor to Tempelhof; C-54s you base in the British zone, and all British aircraft, fly the shorter northern corridor to Gatow and, once it is built, Tegel. Everyone returns through the central corridor.

      Slots. Each airfield lands 1,440 ÷ interval aircraft a day in good weather, times a weather factor. Gatow needs four minutes while RAF Dakotas mix with faster types. Tempelhof runs at reduced capacity until its second runway opens. When demand exceeds slots, the heaviest aircraft land first. Stacking in bad weather wastes slots; the one-attempt rule keeps them but sends a missed aircraft home with its load.

      Maintenance. Every 200 C-54 flying hours create one inspection. From November, depots clear the number of inspections Miller reports for each month (January is inferred from his figures); August to October use a notional 150 a month, above Miller's depot counts of 43, 108 and 137, because the model calibrates on it; field inspections clear more at a cost to the in-commission rate. Inspections more than three aircraft overdue ground aircraft until cleared.

      Accidents and politics. The accident rate per flight comes from the airlift's own totals, scaled by weather, fatigue, landing interval and stacking. Political support moves each week with deliveries against what Washington expects, falls with fatal crashes and empty reserves, and moves with events. These scalings are notional.

      Calibration. Flown with the historical choices, the model delivers within 1 percent of the historical total to 12 May 1949 across 20 weather seeds. It runs about 15 percent behind history in July 1948 and 15 to 27 percent ahead in November and December, when the real airlift also lost aircraft and time to problems the model leaves out. On the two record days it delivers about 6,900 tons for Air Force Day (history 6,989) and about 10,000 for the Easter Parade (history 12,941).

      What the model leaves out: ground transport to the airfields, loading crews, fuel supply, the French contribution, Sunderland flying boats, outbound cargo and passengers, Soviet harassment in the corridors, and much of the politics. It is a teaching model of the operation's bottlenecks, not a reconstruction.

      The historical record

      MonthTonsPer dayFlightsCoal

      USAFE summary, p. 12 (U.S. short tons). June 1948 covers 26–30 June. The airlift continued after the blockade ended and delivered 2,325,509.6 tons in 277,569 flights by the end of September 1949.

      Where the sources differ. For the Easter Parade, Miller gives 12,941 tons in 1,383 flights, the figure painted on the last C-54; the AlliiertenMuseum gives 12,940 tons in 1,398 flights. Miller's text also calls it April 18 once and misnames the weekdays; it ran from noon on 15 April to noon on 16 April 1949. The RAF's history page gives "roughly 400,821 tonnes" without saying what the figure covers; the USAFE summary credits British aircraft with 541,936.9 short tons, including 146,980 by civil charter firms.
