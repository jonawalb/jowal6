Stop the chips

You run US export enforcement for advanced AI chips. A diversion network moves chips through front companies at transshipment hubs and rents cloud compute for restricted end users. Each quarter you spend a budget of 10 and your political capital on enforcement tools. The network adapts: it registers new shells, shifts volume to quieter hubs, splits shipments and rents more cloud. You are scored on two things: how much compute you keep from restricted users, and how little you cost legitimate trade, US industry and allies.

        Case files you drew

        How to read it

        The map shows one quarter. Lines from the left carry what the network tries to move through each hub; lines to the right carry what reaches restricted end users. The orange ring around a hub is the share seized there. Small diamonds are front companies: a hub needs two live ones to run at full volume. While you choose, the map and the numbers show the expected result of your choices for this quarter, before the network adapts. After you end the quarter, the network moves and the map rewires.

        How the game works
        • Budget and capital. 10 budget a quarter, which does not carry over. Political capital starts at 40 and grows by 6 a quarter. It falls by 3 a quarter while industry revenue sits below 92. Licensing relaxations and prosecutions add capital.
        • Routes and risk. Restricted users want 100 compute units in quarter 1, rising by 4 each quarter. The network splits that demand across hubs and cloud by a logit on risk (steepness 4), capped by capacity. End-use checks, hub pressure and agents raise the share seized. Checks and pressure work only as well as the host cooperates, which tracks allied goodwill.
        • Adaptation. After every quarter the network replaces listed shells (85% chance each, less with standing agents) and sometimes at a different hub. A hub where risk passes 18% loses a quarter of its volume to a cooler hub, which can wake dormant Hub D. Heavy end-use checks make it split shipments, which lowers seizures but slows it down. When physical routes get harder it rents more cloud compute. Once a cloud KYC rule bites, some rented compute moves offshore; once location verification bites, diverters learn to spoof it.
        • Lags. A cloud KYC rule takes effect 2 quarters after you start it; location verification 3 quarters. Agents need 2 quarters before prosecutions can land.
        • Score. 65 points times the share of diversion you prevented compared with no enforcement on the same seed, plus 35 points times (1 minus collateral cost / 120). Collateral cost adds friction on legitimate trade, 1.5 times the revenue lost below 100, and goodwill lost below 65.
        • Computer strategies on the end chart: Whack-a-mole lists every identified shell and checks the two busiest hubs with 1 agent level. Rules first relaxes licensing to fund a cloud KYC rule and location verification, then lists shells. Balanced keeps 2 agent levels, presses the busiest hub, starts cloud KYC when it can, lists shells and checks the busiest hub. Everything does all of it every quarter. In 300 test games of 10 quarters: no enforcement scores 35; whack-a-mole about 48 (cuts 35%); rules first about 48 (40%); balanced about 48 (48%, but costly); everything about 35 (54%, very costly). Steady agents at level 3 and nothing else score about 51 (cut 24%, no collateral cost), better than all four named strategies: in this model standing enforcement capacity costs legitimate trade nothing. The best rule found, 2 agent levels plus an early cloud KYC rule plus targeted listings and checks, scores about 53 (cut 48%). Figures from scripts/balance.mjs.
