Double Envelopment

A double envelopment holds the enemy in place from the front while two forces close round both flanks and meet behind. This bench vise shows the part that is easy to miss: the jaws only close round the block while the ram holds it.

This section follows the view you are on. Switch views with the buttons under the card or the V key; the same model and readout drive both. Reset puts the figure back where it started.

How to read it: the machine

What is on the bench

  1. The block in the middle is the enemy force. It sits on a pallet that can slide forward and back in a slot. The ticks round its rim mark its perimeter.
  2. The ram on the straight rail is the fixing force. Its curved pad presses the block’s front and holds it home.
  3. The two jaws are the enveloping forces. Each pivots on one of the block’s flanks and swings shut round its side and rear; the tail rollers run on the arc slides with their scales.
  4. The gauge reads the share of the block’s rim under attack: rim that a jaw or the pad touches.

How it moves

  1. The jaws. Close them with the slider, the arrow keys or by moving the pointer across the figure.
  2. The gauge. Held, with the jaws shut, it reads 78%.
  3. Release the front (button, click or F) and the pad lifts off but stays on its rail. Close the jaws again: with nothing holding it, the block slips out the back through the gap between the jaw tails before they meet, and the gauge drops to 0%.

How to read it: the battlefield

Here the scale is a platoon attack, blue is friendly and red is the enemy. The Army’s platoon manual says that at platoon and company level “the enveloping force’s main effort is focused on the enemy’s flank while the supporting effort of the envelopment usually engages the enemy’s front by fire” (ATP 3-21.8, para. 4-15).

What is on the ground

  1. The enemy. On the rise, an enemy platoon holds two-man fighting positions all round the summit, with a machine-gun nest and a belt of wire on its front; the red fan on the ground is the machine gun’s sector.
  2. The support-by-fire element. On the right, behind a stone wall and inside the dashed support-by-fire position, a rifle squad and a machine-gun team fix the enemy with fire on its front: the blue tracers, strikes and drifting smoke.
  3. The enveloping forces are two rifle squads, each “two fire teams and a squad leader” (para. 1-9); the near one, with the platoon leader and his radio operator, is lit as the main effort.

How it moves

  1. The controls. Move the enveloping squads round the flanks with the Envelopment slider, the arrow keys or by moving the pointer across the figure. Release the front, or fix it again, with the button, a click on the figure or F.
  2. The envelopment. The jaw slider moves them round both flanks in squad column with their teams in wedges, “the squad’s main formation for movement unless preparing for an assault” (para. 3-53), until they turn in and close on the flanks and rear in squad line, a formation “used to assault or as a pre-assault formation” (para. 3-54).
  3. The front held. While the front is held, the enemy stays in its holes under fire.
  4. Release the front and the support-by-fire element ceases fire but stays in position; as the squads close, the unfixed enemy leaves its positions by a sunken lane out the back and gathers beyond it, and the squads close on empty holes (the readout’s 0%).
  5. Losses are notional: greyed figures lying still are men down on both sides, the blue ones carried back to a collection point behind the support-by-fire position where a medic kneels, and the readout counts them, friendly vs enemy, the enemy losing more the more of its rim is under attack and the squads losing more when the front is not held.

Soldiers are drawn larger than scale (about one and a half times) so they read; sizes, ranges, timings, the enemy’s strength and its way out are notional.

What doctrine and history say

U.S. Army FM 3-90, Tactics (May 2023) treats the double envelopment as a variation of the envelopment “where forces simultaneously attack along both flanks of an enemy force.” A unit performs one “by conducting a frontal attack to fix enemy forces in place while enveloping both of the enemy force’s flanks” (para. 2-60).

The fixing force “normally conducts a frontal attack to fix enemy forces in their current positions to prevent their escape and reduce their capability to react against the enveloping force,” and the only organizational difference from a single envelopment “is that a double envelopment consists of two enveloping forces” (para. 2-62). That is the ram’s job in the model: without it, the block slips out.

The classic case is Cannae (August 216 BCE), which, Encyclopaedia Britannica notes, military historians regard as “a classic example of a victorious double envelopment.” In Polybius’s account, the Roman legions pressed into Hannibal’s centre until they had his African infantry on both flanks, and were “caught between the two divisions of the enemy” (Histories 3.115).

What is notional

The geometry is computed, not drawn by hand: a rim point counts as under attack when a jaw face or the pad face lies within 1 unit of it, checked at every degree. With the jaws open the pad alone covers 24% of the rim; shut, the jaws add their share for 78% in all. The mapping is a teaching device, and so is the escape rule: an unheld block slides 57 units out the back once the jaws close past 50° open, and the jaws cannot close through it (a real enemy might fall back, break out or counterattack instead). No percentage here describes a real battle.
