                How to read it

                Meeting replay. Everyone who speaks in the record sits at the table, the President at the head. Pick an issue, then play the meeting turn by turn. A seat fills with its side the first time that person is coded on the issue: blue for side 1, orange for side 0 (the issue card says what each side argued). A grey dashed seat was asked for a view and gave none (N/A). A double ring means the person was coded on both sides during the meeting. The strip above the player shows every turn; tall marks are coded turns.

                Timeline. Every meeting by year, coloured by whether the coders found in-room disagreement. People gives each speaker's record: meetings, sides taken, how often their side was the hawk side, and how often the President's lean matched theirs. Disagreement network joins people coded on opposite sides of the same issue. Patterns aggregates the issue index by administration and period.

                Method & sources

                What the data is. Cheap Talk is Jonathan Walberg's row-level coding of disagreements in U.S. National Security Council and White House meeting records, built with Joe Ruggiero and Matt Malis. Its question is whose advice the President takes. Each meeting record is split into speaker turns (segment IDs read meeting.section.turn). For each disagreement the coders open an issue, write the question with what side 1 and side 0 say, and code every turn that takes a side as 1, 0 or N/A. The tool shows two finished deliveries: SRS1 (110 meetings, 98 issues, delivered 26 July 2026 and frozen) and SRS2 (110 meetings, 106 issues, completed 9 August 2026 and revised on 10 August against the research assistants' coding). Together: 220 meetings from March 1948 to January 1983, 204 issues, 7,538 turns, 1,228 of them coded.

                What counts as an issue. All four must hold: named people on opposing sides; they contest a decision, or a fact or assessment that bears on one; hawk–dove or fear of communism can be answered (including "neither applies"); and it makes sense to ask which side the President came down on. Sub-questions of one fight are one issue. A meeting's code is 1 when there is in-room disagreement. "Level 2" issues record the President asking for advice when nobody disagreed in the room; those meetings stay code 0 but carry issues (8 meetings here).

                The issue codes. Fact/Policy: F if the dispute is about recovering a fact or assessment, P if it is about what to do. About communism: whether the issue concerns communism. Side A/C: which side is worried about looking soft on communism; left blank when every principal is anti-communist and they split only on means, timing or legality (most Cuban Missile Crisis operational votes). For arms control, summitry or trade with the Soviets, the restraining side carries it. Hawk/Dove and Side H: whether the split is hawk against dove, and which side is the hawk. POTUS SP: 1 only when the President voiced an opinion and came down on a side; President Side is then that side. Asking questions or framing without committing is 0. Vice Presidents (Nixon under Eisenhower, Johnson under Kennedy) are coded as themselves, never as the President.

                How the tool computes things. A person's side on an issue is the side of their last coded turn on it; people coded on both sides are marked as having moved (44 person-issue cases). The President's lean always comes from the issue index, never from his turns (they agree in 139 of 140 checkable cases; in 247a Eisenhower is coded on both sides and the index records side 1). "Opposite sides" in the network counts issues where two people ended on different sides. Hawk share is the share of a person's sides on hawk–dove issues that were the hawk side. Speaker roles (State, Defense, Military and so on) are the tool's grouping of the source position titles by keyword; three people who appear under two spellings were merged (J. William Fulbright, Paul "Pete" McCloskey, Benjamin Gilman), and case-only differences were merged.

                Limits. The sample is not every NSC meeting: it is the SRS draws of the project corpus, heavy on the Eisenhower years (87 meetings) and thin on Carter and Reagan (10). Only 10 meetings name Vietnam in their subject or notes; the project's Vietnam series (about 370 meetings) has research-assistant coding and an automated audit that are not yet validated, so it is not shown. SRS1 and SRS2 were coded under slightly different calibrations (SRS1 recall-first; SRS2 re-coded to the research-assistant rate), and SRS2 has open review items: three Side A/C rulings flagged in the issue comments and an unresolved duplicate (meeting 22331). The records are memoranda and minutes, not verbatim transcripts; who "spoke" means who the note-taker recorded. Quoted text is the coded turn, cut at 280 characters; uncoded turns carry no text here.

                How to cite. Jonathan Walberg, Joe Ruggiero and Matt Malis, Cheap Talk: coded disagreements in U.S. National Security Council meetings, SRS1–SRS2 (unpublished data, 2026), via Situation Room Replay, jwalberg.com. Cite a turn by meeting ID and segment ID, for example 36.1.5.
