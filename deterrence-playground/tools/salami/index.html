Fictional and notional. An invented shoal, invented states. Every number is illustrative game design, not a prediction.

Choose your side

A handful of marines live on a rusting ship grounded on a disputed shoal. For twelve months, the Coastal State must keep them supplied and the Coast Guard Power wants them gone, without bringing in the Patron, whose defense treaty with the Coastal State is deliberately vague about places like this. Nobody knows where the other’s red line is. You find it only by crossing it.

The computer plays the other side and the Patron. Difficulty changes only how well the computer plays, never the rules or the Patron: on Easy it is erratic, misreads the evidence and never smashes the line; on Hard it reads the evidence sharply and exploits every opening. A good strategy wins about 75% of the time on Easy, 50% on Medium and 30% on Hard, as either side.

    How the game works
    • Each month the Coastal State picks a resupply method, its messaging and whether to push through a block; the Power picks a rung on its ladder (shadow, block, water cannon, ram, board and seize, cordon), its messaging, and whether to hold course or detain crews. Both move at once.
    • Supplies. The garrison starts with 3 months and eats 1.4 a month. At zero it withdraws. Each method has odds of landing its cargo against each rung; weather and the Patron’s ships change them.
    • The Patron’s hidden line. The Patron intervenes when the rung it sees reaches its line. Observers aboard, injuries, detentions and a surge of international sympathy each add a rung; last month’s heavy Coastal provocation takes one off. Below the line it stays silent, voices concern or warns; its monthly statements are noisy hints.
    • The Power’s hidden threshold. Provoke the Power past its threshold and it will probably snap: two rungs up the next month.
    • Normalization. If the Power uses a gray-zone rung (block, water cannon, ram, board) unanswered in 2 of any 6 months, it becomes normal: it then draws far less sympathy and escalation, the Patron sees it as a rung milder, and the Coastal State loses sympathy and credibility at once. Protest, court, journalists, observers or an escort pushing through count as answers; they cost escalation risk and anger the Power. Answering 3 of a normal rung’s last 4 uses, or a Patron warning, pushes it back.
    • Smash the red line. Once a game, from March, either side can go all in. The Power seizes the outpost under a cordon and wins if the Patron stays out; the Coastal State forces a convoy through and invokes the treaty, and wins if the Patron comes. The Patron decides from its hidden line, how far past it the move goes, its credibility, sympathy, escalation risk (high risk makes it cautious) and how many rungs have become normal. You see an estimate built from your own belief, not the truth.
    • Beliefs. The bars show a Bayesian belief, updated every month from what each side does. You only pin a line down by testing it.
    • Endings. The Coastal State wins by holding twelve months without war; the Power by forcing withdrawal without a Patron intervention. A clash at the shoal, more likely when escalation risk is high and the Patron’s ships are there, means everyone loses.

    Tested over 2,000 games per setting. A fixed good strategy (selective answering, careful slicing, smashing only at high odds) won 76% (Coastal State) and 74% (Power) on Easy, 48% and 47% on Medium, and 33% and 30% on Hard. On Medium the computer won 72% against a passive Coastal State and 100% against a passive Power; reckless play won 16% (Coastal State, with 44% clashes) and 22% (Power); smashing in March won 11% and 31%. With the computer on both sides on Medium, the Coastal State won 42%, the Power 45%, and 2% ended in a clash. The method notes (METHOD.md) give every number.
