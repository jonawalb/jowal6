Game

Question:
With honest reports, does a feeling from elsewhere change the weight you give evidence, and does naming its source shrink that?
What you do:
Answer 12 questions about an invented town; in the last six, name your feeling and its source first.
What you will see:
Your weight on each report (λ̂; 1 = what the numbers justify) beside a twin round with identical numbers. The gap is the feeling's pull.
Limits:
Invented rounds, notional scores, no efficacy data. Three pairs per part says nothing about you.

Most prebunking games teach you to spot tricks in a message. Here every report is honest and its strength is a number. The pull comes from outside the message, and the game measures how far it moves the weight you give the evidence.

Presenting this in 3 minutes
  1. Show one round (45 s). Press . Point at the two evidence lines: only the base rate and the report count. The comment thread carries no evidence.
  2. Show a finished result (60 s). Press . In the chart, the Crowd row in Part 1: the open dot (twin) sits at 1, the filled dot at 2.44, so the crowd made the same numbers count for more. The Earlier news row: the reassuring report counted for nothing (0).
  3. Show the test (45 s). Read the sentence at the top of the card: the gap went from +1.02 in Part 1 to +0.75 in Part 2. Then the two Part 2 rows: "channel seen before" is the crowd pull met again (+0.96), "new channels" averages the grievance and body pulls never practised (+0.65). Each is extra distance from 1 versus the twins, so nearer zero means the pull moved the player less. Walberg's model predicts naming the feeling helps even on new pulls; these are invented example answers, not a test of it.
  4. Hand over (15 s). Press and invite the room to play the five-minute version.

Takeaway: honest evidence can still be over- or under-weighted when a feeling from somewhere else is in the room, and pairing each round with a same-numbers twin makes that visible.

Say aloud: the rounds and the example answers are invented and the scores are notional; three pairs per part says nothing about a person, and the transfer result is a model prediction, not a finding.

Everything in the game is invented: the town, the reports, the comments and the headlines. It is a teaching and research prototype with no efficacy data.

Rounds

Loaded rounds and their neutral twins are mixed together. Which is which is shown after each part.

Sound

What the game reads

posterior odds = prior odds × LRλ

LR is the report's stated strength. The game solves for λ̂ from your answer. λ = 1 is the Bayesian weight: you moved exactly as far as the report justifies. The form is from Walberg's unpublished working papers; the scoring built on it is notional.

How to read it

Each round asks a yes/no question about the invented town of Ardley. Two lines are evidence: a base rate ("about 30 in 100 calls like this turn out real") and a report with a stated strength ("2× as likely if it is real"). Bayes' rule turns those into one right answer. Everything else on the card, a comment thread, a drone, an earlier headline, a grievance, a countdown, carries no evidence.

From your answer the game infers λ̂, the weight you put on the report. At 1 you moved as far as the report justifies; above 1 further; between 0 and 1 less far; below 0 the wrong way. Each loaded round has a neutral twin with identical numbers, so the gap between the two is the part of your answer the feeling moved, not your arithmetic.

Part 2 adds three quick steps before each answer: place your feeling on a simplified Affect Grid (Russell, Weiss and Mendelsohn 1989), pleasant to unpleasant and calm to activated, give it a name, and say what on the screen, or in you, produced it. Two of Part 2's pulls are new, to see whether the habit carries over.

How it differs from Bad News

This page used to hold a short game in the style of Bad News, the Cambridge and DROG game in which "players take on the role of a fake news producer and learn to master six documented techniques" (Roozenbeek and van der Linden 2019). Go Viral! and Harmony Square follow the same inoculation design. Borrowed Feelings departs from it on mechanism.

Why the scoring matters: a reanalysis of five studies found that Bad News and Go Viral! "did not improve discrimination, but rather elicited more 'false' responses to all news items (more conservative responding)" (Modirrousta-Galian and Higham 2023). A later signal-detection meta-analysis of 33 experiments (N = 37,025) reached the opposite conclusion: gamified and video interventions "consistently improve discrimination between reliable and unreliable news, without increasing response bias" (Simchon et al. 2026). The question is still open. Either way, here every report is true and correctly stated, so blanket suspicion scores as badly as over-reaction.

What this is and isn't

It is a five-minute demonstration of an idea from Jonathan Walberg's unpublished working papers: that emotion changes how much weight people give evidence, and that a campaign can exploit this without faking anything. The λ form comes from the Sonic Vectors design and the Emotional Priors papers. The Emotional Priors paper predicts that teaching people "to notice when their reactions are being moved" should transfer across topics, while topic-specific hardening should not (its hypothesis H4). It also flags whether people who correctly attribute their arousal are still moved as the model's "most exposed seam." Part 2 is a toy version of both questions.

It isn't a validated measure or an intervention with evidence behind it. There are no efficacy data. Three twin pairs per part is far too few to say anything about a person; Part 2 always follows Part 1, so practice alone could shrink the gap; the score mixes numeracy with feeling; and the loaded rounds also have more alarming topics than their twins, so part of a gap can be the topic. In Walberg's simulation the transfer of affect-awareness follows from how awareness is specified, so it is a prediction, not a result.

What comes from published work

  • Feelings from one source can be misread as information about another. MacCormack and Lindquist (2019) found that hunger made people "experience negative emotions and to negatively judge a researcher, but only when participants are not aware that they are conceptualizing their affective state as emotions." The attribution step borrows this logic. Schwarz and Clore (1983) showed the core move: weather-induced mood coloured judgments of life satisfaction, but the effect vanished when people's attention was drawn to the weather as its cause. Part 2's "place it" step is built on that result.
  • Martel, Pennycook and Rand (2020): "inducing reliance on emotion resulted in greater belief in fake (but not real) news stories compared to a control or to inducing reliance on reason."
  • The feeling grid is a simplified 5 × 5 Affect Grid (Russell, Weiss and Mendelsohn 1989; the original is 9 × 9) on the two dimensions of affect, valence and arousal (Russell 1980). Barrett (2017): "Interoceptive sensations are usually experienced as lower dimensional feelings of affect." Feldman, Bliss-Moreau and Lindquist (2024) review how signals from the body give rise to valence and arousal.
  • Naming the feeling follows research on affect labeling, which "can attenuate our emotional experiences" (Torre and Lieberman 2018), and on emotion differentiation (Kashdan, Barrett and McKnight 2015).
  • The grievance round borrows the fear and anger contrast from appraisal research (Lerner and Keltner 2001: fearful people gave pessimistic risk estimates, angry people optimistic ones). In Walberg's model fear changes the weight λ while anger changes certainty, so the model predicts that a grievance should not move a single answer's λ̂ much. The round tests whether it does anyway. The crowd round draws on social baseline theory (Beckes and Coan 2011) and Walberg's working hypothesis that false stories persist while the group behind them does.

What is notional

  • The town, reports, base rates, report strengths, comments and headlines are invented.
  • λ̂ from one answer, answers clamped to 1–99%, λ̂ capped to −1…4, the distance |λ̂ − 1| capped at 3, the twin gap and the verdict thresholds (±0.15) are this game's choices.
  • The drone (two detuned low oscillators, a tritone, a slow pulse), the 15-second countdown and the share counter are design choices, not calibrated inductions. With sound off, the soundtrack round has no pull, so its pair is shown but left out of the averages.
  • The verdict uses distance from 1, so a pull that cancels an existing over- or under-weighting can read as "closer to 1"; the signed "pull toward yes" row is the direct contrast.
  • The twins share numbers, not topics: a contaminated reservoir or a gas leak is more alarming in itself than a bridge or a library roof.
