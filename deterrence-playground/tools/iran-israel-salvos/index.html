How to read it

Pick an episode at the top. The bars show what Iran launched, by type, for all three attacks at once, so you can compare sizes; the black whisker shows the spread when sources give different totals. The funnel follows one episode from launch to impact, and the orange box lists where sources disagree. The map and the table show which countries helped defend Israel and which interceptors were reported in use.

The panel on the right sets the cost of the defense against the cost of the attack. Unit costs come from cited estimates and are shown as low-to-high ranges. How many interceptors of each kind were fired is mostly not public, so the mix is a notional input you set; where a source gives a count, it is the default.

Why the numbers differ

Most launch counts come from the Israel Defense Forces, sometimes echoed by U.S. officials. Iran rarely gives numbers. Interception rates depend on what is counted: missiles that failed at launch or fell short, drones downed far from Israel by U.S., British, French or Jordanian aircraft, and warheads that landed in open ground may or may not be included. Independent tallies from satellite imagery or impact counts tend to report more hits than official statements do.

What this tool leaves out

Israel's own strikes on Iran (October 2024 and June 2025), the U.S. strikes of 22 June 2025, Hezbollah and Houthi fire, and every attack by Iran's partners are outside its scope except where they were part of the same salvo. It gives country and city level only, as reported, and no impact locations.
