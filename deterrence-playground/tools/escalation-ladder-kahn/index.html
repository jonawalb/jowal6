On Escalation (1965): the 44 rungs

The path, step by step

Each dot is one step of the crisis; height is the rung this tool codes it at. Dashed red lines are Kahn's thresholds. A ladder predicts a steady climb; real crises jump, stall and come back down.

Why the ladder misleads

Davis and Stan (RAND, 1984) note that Kahn "did not require that his ladders be uniquely correct"; he wanted a structure for more rigorous thinking. These critiques say what the metaphor hides.

How to read it

Kahn arranged 44 kinds of action in a nuclear confrontation from mild to catastrophic, grouped them in seven bands, and marked six thresholds where he thought the character of a crisis changes, from "Don't Rock the Boat" at the bottom to "City Targeting" near the top. RAND's Dangerous Thresholds notes that more than half of the 44 rungs involve at least some use of nuclear weapons. The names on this page are Kahn's, quoted exactly.

Pick a crisis on the right and step through it. Each step is a dated event with its source. The blue number on a rung shows which step reached it; the highlighted rung is the current step. Choose a second crisis under Compare with to overlay its path in orange. Click any rung to see which crises reached it.

No crisis here crossed the No Nuclear Use Threshold. Most of them spent their dangerous hours on rungs 5 through 11: shows of force, confrontations, and alerts. Several also moved down, which a ladder drawn as a climb does not show well.

Method and coding

  • Rung, group and threshold names are transcribed from Kahn's figure (On Escalation, 1965, p. 39) as reproduced in RAND R-3235 (1984), Fig. 2.
  • Every event is dated and linked to a source that was opened and checked on 2026-09-29. Placing an event on a rung is marked coding by the author of this tool unless a source makes the placement itself.
  • Kahn wrote the ladder for a U.S.-Soviet confrontation. Applying it to India and Pakistan, North Korea, or Russia in Ukraine stretches it; the coding picks the closest rung name, and the step notes say why.
  • Where one side's move and the other's response happened days apart, they are separate steps. Rungs reflect the most escalatory action in each step, not the whole crisis.
