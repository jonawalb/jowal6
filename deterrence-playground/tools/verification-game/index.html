How to read it

An inspection game has two players. The inspectee has signed a treaty and can comply or cheat. The inspector has limited means to check. If the inspectee always complied, the inspector would stop inspecting; if the inspector never inspected, the inspectee would cheat. So in equilibrium both sides mix, and the model tells you how often.

The quota game spreads a fixed number of inspections over the periods of a treaty. The first chart shows, for every possible quota, the chance a violation happens and the chance it is caught. The bars show the equilibrium period by period. The alarm game looks at one inspection whose measurement is noisy. The curve shows every trade-off between catching a violation and raising a false alarm that the best test can reach; the dot is where equilibrium puts the inspector. The sweep charts move one stake at a time.

The alarm game carries the literature's most useful surprise. Each player's equilibrium strategy is pinned down by the other player's payoffs. A harsher penalty works mainly on the inspector: the inspectee is deterred at a lower detection chance, so the inspector can accept fewer false alarms, and detection falls with them. Cheating falls only through that shift in the threshold. The inspector's own costs, e and a, set the cheating rate directly, and better instruments cut false alarms and cheating together.

How the models work

Quota game. This is the recursive game of Dresher (1962) in the non-zero-sum form of Avenhaus, von Stengel and Zamir, Section 4.1, Figure 4.2. There are n periods and m inspections; the inspectee violates at most once. Payoffs to (inspector, inspectee): complying throughout (0, 0); undetected violation (−1, 1); caught violation (−a, −b), with 0 < a < 1. The tool solves the game backward period by period, each period a two-by-two game with a mixed equilibrium, and then follows equilibrium play forward to get the chance of a violation and of detection. With certain detection its values match Theorem 4.1 exactly: V(n, m) = C(n−1, m) ÷ Σi≤m C(n, i) bm−i for the inspectee and I(n, m) = −C(n−1, m) ÷ Σi≤m C(n, i) (−a)m−i for the inspector. Tool extension: an inspection catches a violation only with the chance you set; a miss scores as an undetected violation.

Alarm game. Section 3.1, Figure 3.1. A legal inspectee can still set off a false alarm, with payoffs (−e, −h), 0 < e < 1, 0 < h < b. Theorem 3.1: the equilibrium false-alarm chance α* solves −h α* = −b + (1 + b) β(α*), and the violation chance is q* = e ÷ (e − (1 − a) β′(α*)), where β is the chance of missing a violation. The tool takes β from the normal test of Section 3.2, equation (3.14): detection 1 − β = Φ(d − q1−α), with d the violation's size in units of measurement noise.

What is notional: every payoff (a, b, e, h), the number of periods, the quota, the detection chance per inspection and the detection strength. The published games fix the structure and the payoff normalization, not the numbers. What the models leave out: repeated violations, inspections the inspector can announce in advance (the leadership games of Section 5), several inspectees, and national technical means that work alongside on-site inspection.

Related work. Maschler (1966) showed that an inspector who announces and commits to a strategy can do better in the non-zero-sum game. Kilgour applied inspection games to a chemical weapons treaty (1990) and to choosing which sites to inspect under an arms control agreement (1992). Neither extension is modeled here.
