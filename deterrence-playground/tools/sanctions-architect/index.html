Design a sanctions campaign

The Target State has crossed a line. You lead a coalition of four partners against it for eight quarters. Each quarter choose up to three measures: which sector, how hard, how to enforce, and how to keep partners on side. The Target adapts: it reroutes trade through neutral hubs, builds evasion networks, counter-sanctions your weakest partner and rallies its public. It concedes only if pain over time outweighs its resolve, and you do not know how resolved it is.

      What to take away

        How the game works
        • Measures. Five sectors (energy exports as a price cap or an embargo; the central bank or major banks; technology export controls; individuals and elites; shipping and insurance), each Light, Medium or Heavy; three enforcement tools (secondary sanctions, maritime enforcement against a shadow fleet, customs data sharing); and coalition diplomacy (reassure, compensate, carve-out, or court an outsider). Up to three changes a quarter; everything stays in force until you change it.
        • Leakage. Pressure slips around sanctions through outsiders (the Neutral Hub and the Transit Port), through members whose commitment is low, and through the Target’s evasion networks. Rerouting makes the outsiders carry more each quarter. Enforcement plugs leaks; each tool has its own cost.
        • Partners. Each partner has a trade share, a cost it tolerates per quarter, and a commitment. Costs above tolerance erode commitment; below 30 a partner may leave and its trade reopens. The Target counter-sanctions the weakest member.
        • Concession. Pain accumulates (older pain fades by a fifth each quarter), is multiplied by credibility (from cohesion), plus elite pressure, minus the rally round the flag. The Target’s hidden type sets how much pressure it needs; demand and ambition multiply that. Each quarter you see your estimate of the odds, averaged over your belief about its type, which you update every quarter it holds out.
        • Score (0–100): the demand achieved, times its ambition value (68 points), coalition cohesion at the end (15), your economic cost (11) and the global price shock (6). You are compared with the computer playing your seat against the same Target.

        All numbers are illustrative game design. In 1,000 test games per row with the computer in your seat: coalition-preserving play won a modest demand 63% of the time (mean score 53) and a maximal demand 19% (mean 36), with no partner defections; everything at once won 71% and 18% but scored 40 and 17 after its costs, price shocks and two to three defections a game; a minimal package won 2% and 1% and scored 31.
