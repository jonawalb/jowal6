Nash equilibrium assumes players never slip. Quantal response equilibrium lets them err, with fewer errors when more is at stake. Turn the precision λ from pure noise to perfect play and watch crisis outcomes change.

Why estimating these games needs care

simulated data

To estimate a game, a researcher solves for its equilibrium at every trial value of the parameters and asks how likely the data are. When a game has several equilibria, the solver has to pick one, and the likelihood can jump. Here the standoff game (its current payoffs) generates simulated crises at a known λ*, and five methods try to recover it.

    How to read it

    In a quantal response equilibrium each player chooses better actions more often, but not always. Under the logit form a player picks action j with probability proportional to eλ·U(j), where U(j) is the expected payoff of j given how everyone else actually plays, errors included. Each player knows the others err and plans for it. The precision λ sets how sharply choices follow payoffs.

    • λ = 0: choices ignore payoffs and every action is equally likely. McKelvey and Palfrey (1995): “when λ = 0, there is a unique equilibrium at the centroid of the simplex.”
    • λ → ∞: play approaches Nash equilibrium. In a game of perfect information, Signorino (1999) notes, “λ = ∞ also corresponds to the subgame perfect equilibrium.” The ∞ column on the path chart shows that limit.
    • In between, QRE is its own prediction, not a blurred Nash equilibrium. At the default payoffs, war in the Signorino game becomes more likely as λ rises from 0 to about 2, then falls away.
    • The tree shows each choice probability at the current λ. Thicker branches are more likely. The boxes give payoffs (player 1, player 2), the outcome’s probability and a bar.
    • The standoff has simultaneous moves, so the logit equations can have several solutions at one λ. The path chart draws every one. The bold curve is the principal branch, which starts at the centroid.
    • The address bar stores the game, λ, every payoff and the simulation settings, so a view can be shared exactly.

    The games

    Signorino crisis game. From Signorino (1999, Figure 2). State 1 fights or not. State 2 then fights or not. If State 1 held back and State 2 fought, State 1 chooses again. Outcomes are the status quo, capitulation by either side, and war. Utilities follow his equations 13–17: the status quo is worth D12 (1 if both states are democracies); a state that capitulates loses its assets Ai; the winner of a capitulation gains the other’s assets; war is worth PiAj + (1 − Pi)(−Ai − Mi), with Pi = Mi/(Mi + Mj). The choice probabilities solve his equations 4–7.

    Deterrence game with audience costs. The move order of the crisis-bargaining game that Lewis and Schultz (2003) estimate and Crisman-Cox and Gibilisco (2021) study: A challenges or accepts the status quo; B resists or concedes; a resisted A fights or backs down. The payoff for backing down, ā, carries the audience cost. Here both sides know all payoffs, so there is no signaling. In their version each side has private payoff shocks, and that is where multiple equilibria come from.

    Crisis standoff (Chicken). Both sides choose at once to escalate or back down. Escalating against a side that backs down wins v; backing down against escalation loses v; mutual escalation costs each side its clash cost. When both clash costs exceed v the game has two pure Nash equilibria and one mixed.

    Why estimation needs care

    Signorino (1999) turned the logit QRE into a statistical model of crisis behavior and warned that “the above is not just another formulation of nested logit”: the choice probabilities “are the equilibria of the decision makers’ best responses to each other.” He also shows that λ cannot be separated from the scale of the payoffs: “Both λ and the β parameters cannot be estimated individually,” so he fixes λ = 1. The “Same data, different scale” box in the panel demonstrates this.

    Crisman-Cox and Gibilisco (2021) show what goes wrong when a game has several equilibria. Traditional maximum likelihood “maximize[s] a likelihood function which computes an equilibrium for every observation at every guess of the parameters,” and “an equilibrium is computed as if it is unique.” Multiple equilibria “create discontinuities in the likelihood function.” In their Monte Carlo study, “the tMLE routine performs the worst in both multiple and unique settings, regardless of the number of observations,” while “the NPL and PL methods consistently perform the best.” They adapt the pseudo-likelihood, nested pseudo-likelihood (Aguirregabiria and Mira 2007) and constrained MLE (Su and Judd 2012) estimators from industrial organization. For extensive-form QRE of the Signorino kind they add a reassurance: “Because these games always have a unique equilibrium, the problems we discuss do not arise in these contexts.”

    The card above reproduces the mechanism in the simplest setting that has it: a 2×2 game with a single unknown, λ. It is a teaching stand-in, not their signaling game. With one parameter, the constrained problem can be solved by brute force: find every equilibrium at every λ and keep the best, which is the objective CMLE maximizes.

    Method

    Trees. The logit agent QRE of a perfect-information game is found by backward induction: the last mover’s logit choice depends only on payoffs, the mover before responds to that noisy behavior, and so on to the root. It is unique at every λ.

    The standoff. A 2×2 QRE solves p = σ(λ·Δ₁(q)) and q = σ(λ·Δ₂(p)), where σ is the logistic function and Δ is a player’s payoff advantage of escalating. Substituting one into the other leaves a single equation in logit p. The tool finds every root on a fine grid with bisection, and traces the whole correspondence for λ from 0.01 to 100 by marching squares on the zero set. The principal branch is the component that reaches the λ = 0.01 edge. McKelvey and Palfrey (1995, Theorem 3) prove that for almost all games “the graph of π* contains a unique branch which starts at the centroid, for λ = 0, and converges to a unique Nash equilibrium, as λ goes to infinity,” and call that limit the limiting logit equilibrium. When the two clash costs are equal the game is symmetric, a non-generic case where the branch reaches a fork; the tool then follows one side arbitrarily.

    Estimation card. For a seed, the tool draws N pairs of uniform numbers once. A crisis’s choices are escalate if the draw falls below the equilibrium probability at λ*, so moving λ* or N changes the data smoothly. “Another equilibrium” uses the solution at λ* farthest from the principal branch. Traditional MLE uses the root nearest the solver’s starting guess. PL uses observed frequencies as beliefs. NPL updates beliefs with one best-response step per round and re-estimates, up to 60 rounds. λ is searched on a log grid from 0.1 to 40 and refined by golden-section search.

    Walberg’s use of QRE

    Jonathan Walberg’s working paper Skin in the Game (model note, July 13, 2026) builds its core estimated model on a three-player tripwire game among a defender, a host and a challenger. The defender resists a challenge with logit quantal-response probability, and the challenger’s choice among graded levels of pressure becomes a structural ordered probit, taken to a weekly panel in Asia. The note gives two reasons for QRE: “every outcome occurs with positive probability, which the likelihood requires; and λ soaks up decision noise in a data-generating process built from bureaucratic reporting of gray-zone events.” It lists λ among the estimated parameters, identified by “sharpness of responses to large shocks.” Walberg’s reading notes on Crisman-Cox and Gibilisco flag the trade-off: a QRE of this kind sidesteps multiple equilibria, but it gives up the belief updating that a signaling model has. The notes plan checks across starting values and solvers at the estimates. A later draft, the data-and-games memo of September 21, 2026, sets out six candidate games, from a one-shot provocation game to a multi-theater portfolio game, and proposes a two-player channel-decomposition game as the workhorse, so the estimated specification is not yet settled.

    What is notional

    • Every payoff value: M, A and D in the Signorino game; W̄, ā and CB (with S = 0, V = 1) in the deterrence game; clash costs and v in the standoff. The forms are from the papers; the numbers are teaching inputs.
    • The simulated crises, N, λ* and the seed. No real crisis data are used.
    • The solver-start rule for traditional MLE is a simple stand-in for “whichever equilibrium an equation solver finds.”
