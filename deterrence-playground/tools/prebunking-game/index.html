    How to read it

    Prebunking tries to protect people before they meet misinformation, by showing them a weakened example of a technique and explaining how it works. The Cambridge Social Decision-Making Lab built a game on this idea, Bad News, in which players act as a fake-news producer and learn six techniques. This page is a short game in the same spirit.

    First you rate nine invented posts on how much you would trust them. Then you run an invented page and, for each technique, pick the post a manipulator would write. The feedback highlights the tells and quotes the technique's definition from the research. Then you rate nine different posts, and the results compare the two rounds.

    The results report two things. Trust in manipulative posts should fall after training. Trust in plain posts should not. Discernment is the gap between the two, and it is the number to watch: rating everything lower is not the same as telling posts apart.

    Method

    Techniques. The six techniques and their definitions come from Roozenbeek and van der Linden (2019), pages 3 and 4: impersonation, emotion, polarization, conspiracy, discrediting and trolling. The paper's abstract calls discrediting "deflecting blame", and its results call the discrediting item "deflection".

    Posts. Every post, account, town and event was written for this game. None comes from the published studies and none refers to a real person or organization. The posts copy the format of the studies, which asked players to rate the reliability of invented posts on a 7-point scale before and after play and included items with no technique as controls. Each test here has six manipulative posts, one per technique, and three plain posts. You get one set before training and the other after, in random order.

    Scoring. Your ratings are averaged separately for manipulative and plain posts. Discernment is the plain average minus the manipulative average. A rating of 1 to 3 counts as flagging a post. The verdict line uses simple rules written for this tool: a drop of at least 0.5 points in trust counts as a change.

    Training meters. Followers and credibility mimic the meters in Bad News but use made-up values. They are marked notional.

    What this game is not. It is not Bad News and has not been validated. Its items have not been tested for difficulty, so the two sets may differ, and nine ratings per test is too few for a reliable score. Your score says nothing about how the published effects apply to you. Nothing you enter leaves your browser.

    What the research found

    These are published results for the original game, quoted exactly. They appear again, with more rows, on the results screen.

    • the average inoculation effect was relatively high, Mpre = 2.61, Mpost = 2.06, Mdiff = −0.55, [95% CI −0.53, −0.57], t(13787) = −61.21, p < 0.001, d = 0.52, Hedges g = 0.43Roozenbeek & van der Linden 2019, p. 7. Self-selected sample, no control group.
    • compared to the control condition, the shift in post-pre difference scores was significantly more negative in the inoculation condition ( M = –0.09 vs M = –0.45, M diff = –0.36, 95% CI [–0.19, –0.52], d = –0.60Basol, Roozenbeek & van der Linden 2020, Results. Randomized, N = 196.
    • Across the studies, when comparable true and fake news items were used, Bad News and Go Viral! did not improve discrimination, but rather elicited more “false” responses to all news items (more conservative responding).Modirrousta-Galian & Higham 2023, abstract (accepted manuscript). A reanalysis of five studies.
