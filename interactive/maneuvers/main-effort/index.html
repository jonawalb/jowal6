Main Effort and Economy of Force

A commander seldom has enough combat power to enjoy a favorable correlation of forces everywhere (FM 3-0). Army doctrine’s answer (ADP 3-0) is to name one main effort, usually weight it with the preponderance of combat power, and resource the rest with the minimum necessary. This bench model shows the trade.

Main effort

This section follows the view you are on. Switch views with the buttons under the card or the V key; the same model and readout drive both. Reset puts the figure back where it started.

How to read it: the machine

What is on the bench

  1. The wall has four sectors, A to D (one to four pips on the tags in front). Each sector is a gate held up by springs and two shear pins, with a stack of weight discs pressing down on it.
  2. The discs are the force’s combat power: twelve in all, never more. The loader on the overhead beam carries them from gate to gate, up to four at a time.
  3. The lit gate is the main effort.

How it moves

  1. The main effort. Pick it by clicking a gate, with the buttons or with the left and right arrow keys. The slider (or the up and down keys) sets how many discs each supporting gate keeps.
  2. In this model, a gate with six or more discs shears its pins and drops through the wall: a breach. A gate with one to five discs stays held. A gate with none is pushed back up by its springs: an exposed sector.

Try three settings. Spread evenly, three discs each: every sector holds and nothing breaks. One disc on each supporting gate, nine on the main effort: the main effort breaks through and the rest still hold. None on the supporting gates: the breach comes, but three sectors are left exposed.

How to read it: the battlefield

Here the scale is a rifle company attacking on a front of four platoon sectors, A to D from left to right, blue friendly and red enemy.

What is on the ground

  1. The enemy. Across the top, the enemy holds two-man fighting positions, two in each sector, with a wood at its back.
  2. The blue elements. Each disc on the bench is one blue element on the ground: a rifle squad, “two fire teams and a squad leader” (ATP 3-21.8, para. 1-9), each team built around a team leader, an automatic rifleman, a grenadier and a rifleman (para. 1-11); a machine-gun team, “a gunner and an assistant gunner” (para. 1-13); or an attached tank. Each sector starts with a squad, a machine-gun team and a tank, and gives up its tank and machine-gun team before its squad.
  3. The lit sector is the main effort.
  4. The markings. The line of departure is the chalk line on pegs, with chalk boundaries between the sectors; the flag marks the company’s objective.

How it moves

  1. The main effort. You can click a sector of the ground to make it the main effort, or use the A to D buttons or the left and right arrow keys. The slider (or the up and down keys) sets how many elements each supporting sector keeps.
  2. Weighted with six or more elements, its tanks drive through the enemy line and its squads follow in squad column, while the thinly held sectors stay on their line, down and firing; machine-gun teams stay where they are, and stop firing once no enemy is left in front of them.
  3. Spread three to a sector, every sector stalls in squad line, a formation “used to assault or as a pre-assault formation” (para. 3-54), trading fire at the same distance.
  4. Empty. A sector left with nothing is empty, and the enemy there comes out of its positions into the open ground; its red fan marks the empty ground its fire now covers, beside the open flank of the next sector.
  5. Shifting. Elements shifted between sectors (the bench’s loader) travel along the road behind the line of departure.
  6. Losses. Success is not free: greyed figures lying still are men down on both sides, the first blue one at a collection point by the road with a medic kneeling beside him; the readout ends with the count, blue against red.

Soldiers and tanks are drawn larger than scale (about one and a half times) so they read; sizes, ranges, timings, the enemy’s strength, the mix of elements and the casualty counts are notional.

What doctrine says

U.S. Army ADP 3-0, Operations (31 July 2019) defines the main effort as “a designated subordinate unit whose mission at a given point in time is most critical to overall mission success. It is usually weighted with the preponderance of combat power. Typically, commanders shift the main effort one or more times during execution” (para. 4-38). The loader stands for that shift: the tour moves the main effort from C to B. A supporting effort is “a designated subordinate unit with a mission that supports the success of the main effort,” and commanders, the manual says, “resource supporting efforts with the minimum assets necessary to accomplish the mission” (para. 4-39).

FM 3-0, Operations (1 October 2022) lists economy of force among the nine principles of war: “Expend minimum-essential combat power on secondary efforts to allocate the maximum possible combat power on the main effort.” The same table defines mass: “Concentrate the effects of combat power at the most advantageous place and time to produce decisive results” (table 1-1). The manual calls economy of force “a reciprocal of mass” that “requires deliberately accepting risk in selected areas to achieve relative superiority—or overwhelming effects—in others” (para. A-10). Joint doctrine uses nearly the same words, with “primary efforts” in place of “the main effort” (JP 3-0, appendix A, para. 2e).

What is notional

Every number on the bench is a teaching value, not data: twelve discs, a breach at six discs on one gate, at least one disc to hold a sector, a held gate that sinks up to 2 units on its springs and an exposed one that rises 6. With these values, spreading the discs evenly never breaks through and weighting one gate does; that is true in this model because the thresholds were set that way. Real combat power does not add up like weights, sectors differ in strength, and doctrine’s “minimum” depends on the mission and the enemy, not on a fixed count.

The casualty counts in the readout and the Battlefield view are notional too, set so that success is never free and spreading out is not safe. A held sector loses one man for every two elements in it (rounded up), so a sector that commits more to an attack that stalls loses more; a breaching sector loses 2; each exposed sector costs 2 more in the nearest occupied sector, from fire into its open flank. The enemy loses 3 of the 4 men in a breached sector and 1 in a held sector with three or more elements against it. Spread evenly, that is 8 down against 4 with no breach; nine on the main effort and one on each other sector, 5 against 3 with a breach; everything on one sector, 8 against 3 with a breach and three sectors exposed. These are teaching values, not loss rates from data or doctrine.
