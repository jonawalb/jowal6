Ten years to cut the cord

It is 2027. You run the critical-minerals policy of an import-dependent industrial democracy. Most of the lithium, graphite, magnet rare earths, gallium, germanium and cobalt your industry uses is refined by one dominant Supplier. Each year you get 10 budget points and some political capital. Fund mines and refineries, buy stockpiles, sign allied offtakes, invest in recycling and substitutes, reform permitting, or rally other buyers. The Supplier watches what you do. It may license or ban exports, or dump prices to sink your new projects.

  • Money cannot buy time. A mine takes years, often longer than the game.
  • Refining is the chokepoint. Ore from friendly mines still has to be processed somewhere.
  • Stockpiles buy time only. They cover a cutoff for months, not a decade.

        Three lessons, in your numbers

          How the game works
          • Each year an event lands, you plan up to four actions within your budget and political capital, then the Supplier responds. Projects advance, then each mineral settles: shortfall = demand − (secure supply + what the Supplier still delivers + stockpile draw). Shortfalls cut an industry output index, weighted by how much each mineral matters.
          • Projects draw a lead time when funded and can slip, meet local opposition, fail, or be abandoned if the Supplier dumps prices (unless you pay for a price floor). Refining only adds secure supply where you have ore to feed it.
          • The Supplier is commercial, coercive or opportunist, drawn in secret. Tension (raised by your projects, coalition-building and events) and how exposed you look make controls likelier; a buyers’ coalition makes them rarer, shorter and less biting. Your read of its type is Bayes’ rule over its moves.
          • Score: supply security 35%, resilience in 2036 (what would survive a total, sustained cutoff) 30%, industry output 20%, cost discipline 15%.
