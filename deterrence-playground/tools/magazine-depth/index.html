Fictional and notional. An invented country and an invented war. Every cost, production rate, lead time and wartime use rate is illustrative game design, not data.

You run the munitions industry

It is 2027. For up to ten years you set your country's munitions budget: what to buy from the lines you have, which lines to expand (they take two to four years to arrive), which sub-tier suppliers to back up, whether to sign multiyear contracts, train workers, co-produce with a partner or develop new types. Somewhere in those ten years, maybe, a war starts. You will not know when. Watch the warnings.

When it comes, the war is fought week by week from what you built. Stocks drain far faster than any line can refill them. You choose how hard to fire each class. You are scored on how many days the line holds, how ready you were and how well you spent.

    1. Working it out…
    How the game works
    • Each year you get a budget (it grows a little when warnings rise: the money comes when it is hardest to spend). You pay only for what is delivered; money a bottleneck stops you spending is lost.
    • Lines make at most their capacity a year. An expansion takes two to four years to arrive. Trained workers raise output and, in war, how far a line can surge.
    • Sub-tier suppliers (energetics, rocket motors, seekers and microelectronics, castings and forgings) are shared across lines. Their real capacity is hidden until you audit them or they run short. A second source adds capacity after a lead time and softens disruptions.
    • Multiyear contracts cut unit cost and commit you to a minimum order for five years. Two years in, the demand signal leads suppliers to grow that line and its component makers at their own expense.
    • Co-production with a partner adds cheaper output that needs none of your components, but the partner may keep its share, in peace and in war.
    • New types count for more in war than old ones, which lose value as the threat moves on; the line slows for two years while it changes over.
    • War runs up to 26 weeks. Each class has a weekly demand. Production surges only from lines and suppliers already built, and enemy strikes hit suppliers. The line holds while the front gets what it needs; it gives ground fast when a class runs dry. Below 35 is critical shortfall and the war is lost. If no war comes, the computer fights a stress test with what you built.
    • Score: days sustained (60 points), readiness when the war began (25: weeks of stock, second sources and workforce) and peacetime cost efficiency (15: list-price value delivered per dollar). The benchmark is the computer playing your seat.

    All numbers are illustrative. In 1,000 computer games per strategy: buying stock only scored a median of 71 (the line held a median of 119 days); expanding capacity first, 71 (126 days, but only 77 when the war came after three years); the balanced computer, 81 (140 days). No strategy held all 182 days. No war came in about 20% of games.

    Real-world orders of magnitude, from public reporting: in CSIS war games the United States ran out of some long-range precision munitions in less than a week of a Taiwan Strait conflict; replacing major inventories at surge rates was estimated to take years; some missiles take about two years to produce; and new factory capacity takes 18 to 24 months or more (Seth G. Jones, Empty Bins in a Wartime Environment, CSIS, January 2023). The game's own numbers are not drawn from that report.
