What the meta-analysis found

Walter & Tukachinsky 2020 · 32 studies, N = 6,527

How to read it

Each dot is one of 200 people who took in a false story. After the correction, a person either revises or keeps the belief. The big number is the share who keep it.

  • The retraction condition chart shows why. Every person has a bar, the value of keeping the belief: the identity weight (1 − α) times what the claim means to them, UI, plus the cost of abandoning it, C. The correction has one strength, α(E) times its evidence. People whose bar falls below that line revise.
  • Timing, repetition change the cost C. Source, an alternative explanation and restating the myth change the evidence. Emotion changes α. Identity shifts every UI.
  • The waterfall starts from the average correction (31%) and adds your choices one at a time, in a fixed order.
  • The forest plot is data, not model: the subgroup estimates from Walter and Tukachinsky (2020), exactly as reported.
  • The address bar stores every setting, so a design can be shared.

What the evidence says

  • Corrections help but rarely erase. Walter and Tukachinsky: “Aggregation of results from 32 studies (N= 6,527) revealed that, on average, correction does not entirely eliminate the effect of misinformation (r = -.05, p = .045).” Chan, Jones, Hall Jamieson and Albarracín (k = 52, N = 6,878) report “large effects for presenting misinformation (ds = 2.41–3.08), debunking (ds = 1.14–1.33), and the persistence of misinformation in the face of debunking (ds = 0.75–1.06).”
  • Timing. “The continued effect of misinformation was stronger when the correction was presented after a filler task (r = -.13, CI [-.28, -.02], p = .045) compared to misinformation that was corrected immediately (r =.01, CI [-.05, .06], p = .87).” The studies coded delay only as immediate or after a filler task. On longer spans, Ecker et al. (2022): “The beneficial effects of debunking can last several weeks, although the effects can wear off quicker.”
  • Source. “the continued influence effect was weaker when the same source delivered both the correction and the misinformation (r = .01, CI [-.06, .08], p = .74) and stronger effects were observed when different sources were responsible for misinformation and its correction (r = -.10, CI [-.18, -.01], p = .02).” No meta-analysis in this set codes in-group against out-group sources. Ecker et al.: “people tend to trust sources that are perceived to share their values and worldviews,” and “corrections are more impactful when they come from a socially-connected source (for example, a connection on social media) rather than a stranger.”
  • Repetition. “repetition of misinformation is a significant predictor of the continued influence effect (b = -.30, SE = .12, p = .001), with each additional repetition of misinformation enhancing its continued influence on beliefs by an average of .30 points.” Restating the myth inside the correction was not significant (r = -.10 against -.02, p = .18). Ecker et al.: “misinformation familiarity contributes to the CIE but does not typically produce backfire effects.”
  • Alternative explanation. Corrections coded as coherent, which “had to mention a reason for the distribution of the misinformation or provide an alternative explanation, rather than merely retract the misinformation,” left r = -.03 against -.12 for bare retractions. Walter and Murphy (2018): “appeals to coherence (r = .55) outperform fact-checking (r = .25), and appeals to credibility (r = .14).” Ecker et al.: “the most important element of a debunking correction is to provide a factual account that ideally includes an alternative explanation for why something happened.” One complication: Chan et al. found that “a detailed debunking message also correlated positively with the misinformation-persistence effect.”
  • Emotion. The meta-analyses do not show that emotional misinformation is harder to correct. Walter and Tukachinsky found “no significant difference in the continued influence effect (Q(1) = 0.04, p = .85) between negatively valanced misinformation … and neutral or positive misinformation.” Chan and Albarracín (2023) found “corrections were more effective for negative (versus neutral) misinformation (b(60) = 0.27, P = .017, 95% CI 0.05 to 0.50).” Ecker et al. describe a study that “found no impact of misinformation emotiveness on the magnitude of the CIE,” while noting that misinformation conveying fear or anger “might be particularly likely to evoke a CIE.”
  • Worldview. “counter-attitudinal corrections led to stronger continued influence effects (r = -.20, CI [-.32, -.07], p = .002), compared with pro-attitudinal corrections.” Walter, Cohen, Holbert and Morag (2020) find fact-checking’s effect (d = 0.29) “substantially attenuated by participants’ preexisting beliefs, ideology, and knowledge.”

Method · the Sticky Affect condition

Jonathan Walberg’s Sticky Affect model (working paper) holds that people trade accuracy against identity. “Belief updating requires: α(E) · Evidence Strength > (1 − α(E)) · Identity Utility + C,” where α is the weight on accuracy, E is emotional activation with dα/dE < 0, and C = g(N, S) is the cost of abandoning the belief, rising in narrative coherence N and social reinforcement S. “When this inequality fails, belief persistence emerges.” The model also states that “Corrective information reduces belief persistence only when it provides a coherent replacement narrative,” which is why the alternative-explanation lever acts on evidence. The same condition drives corrections in this site’s Misinformation Cascade tool.

Method · combining the evidence

Everything that turns these effect sizes into one number is notional, this tool’s own construction. No study estimates it.

  • Each lever’s shift is Δz = atanh(rlevel) − atanh(−.05), the Fisher-z distance between Walter and Tukachinsky’s subgroup estimate and their overall estimate. Repetition adds −.30 per extra hearing, treating their slope as a z shift.
  • Evidence = e0·exp(κ·ΣΔz) over source, alternative explanation and restating. Cost C = C0·exp(−κ·ΣΔz) over timing and repetition. κ = 2.5 by default.
  • α(E) = 1/(1 + E), the same form as the Misinformation Cascade tool. The paper fixes only that α falls as E rises.
  • The 200 people differ only in identity value, UI, spread evenly on a logistic scale around the mean you set.
  • e0 is solved so that the average correction (all Δz = 0, E = 1, identity 1) leaves 31% believing: Chan et al.’s random-effects persistence d = 0.97 divided by their misinformation effect d = 3.08.
  • The moderators are treated as independent and additive. Meta-analytic subgroups are not: they overlap and are confounded, so this adds effects the literature reports one at a time.

What is missing

  • No meta-analysis here measures delay in hours, days or weeks. “After a delay” means a filler task in a lab.
  • No meta-analysis here codes in-group against out-group correctors. The same-source contrast is the closest available.
  • No pooled estimate shows that emotion makes corrections fail. The emotion effect in this tool comes from the Sticky Affect theory alone.
  • Repeated corrections could not be tested: Walter and Tukachinsky note “the lack of variance in our sample.”
