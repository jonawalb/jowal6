How to play

A submarine is hiding in the glowing ring north of the Faroes and trying to slip out into the Atlantic. Each hour, pick Sonobuoys or the Patrol aircraft, click the map to search there, then press End hour. The glow is your probability map: brighter means more likely, and it updates after every search. When you think you know where the sub is, press Attack and click that spot. You have one shot and 24 hours.

    How it works

    The goal. A report puts a submarine somewhere inside the dashed ring. It is heading for one of three gaps into the Atlantic. You have 24 hours to find it, 6 sonobuoy drops, 3 aircraft flights and one attack. If it reaches an exit, or time runs out, you lose.

    Each hour. Use at most one tool: drop sonobuoys (16 buoys in a circle that listen for 6 hours) or send the patrol aircraft (it searches a square for 2 hours). Your towed-array ship costs nothing and steers itself toward the brightest part of the map, listening as it goes. Then press End hour.

    The glow. The orange glow is your probability map. It spreads each hour because the sub moves. When a sensor searches an area and hears nothing, the odds there drop, and because the sub must be somewhere, the odds everywhere else rise. A contact pulls the odds toward it, but some contacts are noise. Best attack odds is the most probability your map puts inside any one attack ring.

    Afterwards. The reveal draws the sub's true track, marks which contacts were real, and charts how much of your map sat near the sub each hour. Move the slider to replay the hunt. The link in the address bar replays your exact hunt.

    Method and notional values

    Detection follows classical search theory. The sonobuoys and the aircraft use Koopman's random search formula, P = 1 − e−WL/A, where W is the sweep width, L the length of search track and A the area (Koopman 1946, p. 28). For sonobuoys, which sit still, the sub's own speed supplies the track length. The towed array uses a lateral range curve, the chance of detection against how close the sub passes, whose area is the sweep width (Koopman, p. 24).

    The probability map is a particle filter: 4,000 possible submarines, each moved by the same rules as the real one, each reweighted every hour by Bayes' rule given what your sensors did and did not hear. Stone et al. (2014) used the same representation in the Air France 447 search, and name the 1968 search for USS Scorpion as an earlier success of the Bayesian approach. The opening ring is a version of the "flaming datum" problem: the longer you wait, the larger the area the sub could be in (Washburn 1980).

    Your map uses the true rules and the true sprint rate. Real searchers never have that. The only unknowns here are the dice, the route and, if you pick "Unknown" under Advanced, the behaviour. Treat your score as an upper bound.

    The coastlines are Natural Earth. Everything tactical is notional: the sensors, ranges and sweep widths, the aircraft and ship performance, the sub's speeds and routes, the start area, false-contact rates and the attack radius. None describes a real system, patrol area or operation. The table lists every value.

    Left out: sound propagation, thermal layers and weather; ice; the sub's own sonar and weapons; real surveillance networks; allied and opposing forces; and the time needed to attack. The game teaches how search reasoning works, not how any real hunt would go.

    A simple automatic hunter plays 50 hunts at each of four sprint rates against the behaviour set under Advanced. It drops sonobuoys every fourth hour and sends the aircraft every eighth, each on the brightest water not already covered, and attacks once its best attack odds reach 50% (or on the last hour).
