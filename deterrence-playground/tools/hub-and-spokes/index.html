Fictional and notional. Invented allies and an invented Rival. Every number is illustrative game design, not a prediction.

You are the hub

Four allies depend on you, each through its own bilateral treaty, and none of them much trusts the others. A Rival watches for weak links. For ten years you spend a limited budget of political capital to keep them with you.

The trap: allies that doubt you hedge, toward the Rival or toward a deterrent of their own (abandonment). Allies that are too sure of you stop paying and start taking risks, and you may be dragged into their quarrels (entrapment). Each ally has a comfort zone you cannot see directly.

        How the game works
        • Each year an event may shake one ally’s politics or your own, and the Rival probes the allies it thinks you care least about (exposed allies, low assurance, no forces present). Then you spend about 10 points of political capital across the four allies.
        • Reassure (forward deployment, summit, joint exercise, guarantee statement, arms transfer), restrain (private warning, conditioning aid, refusing arms), press for burden-sharing, or link two allies directly. Guarantees, arms and pressure ask how far to go.
        • Allies react to how assured they feel against a hidden comfort zone that depends on their exposure and their fear of abandonment. Below it they lose cohesion and hedge; above it they cut their own defense effort and, if risk-loving, start provoking the Rival.
        • Crises come from Rival probes or from emboldened allies. You back the ally fully, back it with restraint, or stay out. Every ally watches: staying out of a probe costs credibility everywhere, more where you made public or treaty-level pledges. Allies tied to each other feel the shock less.
        • Budget strain from deployments, arms and crises cuts next year’s capital.
        • Score: 30% alliance cohesion, 30% wars avoided, 20% burden sharing, 20% hub credibility. The computer then plays your same ten years 60 times, with slightly different reads of the allies, and you see where you rank.

        In 1,000 test games per strategy: the computer hub averaged 83; reassuring everyone as much as possible averaged 52 (2.3 wars a game; burden sharing collapses); restraining everyone averaged 57 (cohesion near zero); doing nothing averaged 42. Numbers are illustrative.
