Notional model

Question: Can one correction stop a small true report from snowballing into an alarming false claim, and does its timing matter more than its messenger?

What you do: Press Play, then set when, how widely and from whom a correction goes out.

What you will see: Dots coloured by the version each person passes on, and a chart of people sharing with your correction (solid) and without it (dashed).

Limits: Teaching values on one synthetic 160-person network. It shows what the assumptions imply, not any real audience.

An accurate report of 16 aircraft becomes “the island is surrounded” one retelling at a time. Set how easily people share, how frightened they are, and when a correction arrives.

Presenting this in 3 minutes
  1. Plays from round 0 to 40. Say: four people see an accurate report; by round 40, 84 of 159 sharers pass on “circling” or “surrounded”. Say: the tipping point; half the network shares (90 of 160), and 62 of them still pass on the accurate report or “dozens of jets”.
  2. Look at the chart and the panel. Say: 75% still share at round 40, against 99% with none. Only 2 of 31 sharers it reaches retract.
  3. Say: 44% share; all 31 sharers it reaches retract. The dotted line on the chart is the last run. If the headline shows “A blockade has begun”, say why: the correction slows the spread, so new people keep joining until round 18 instead of 15, and each new sharer repeats the most alarming version it saw and may step it up. In this run 21 of the 27 people who joined after round 8 and still share carry the blockade version, against 7 of 69 with no correction. That follows from the model’s rule; it is not an observed backfire.
  4. Say: 8% share. An early outsider beat a later insider here.

Takeaway: In this model the claim locks in within a few rounds, so when a correction arrives can matter as much as who sends it.

Say aloud: These are teaching values on one network (seed 7). Across 50 random networks the round-3 outsider leaves a median of 30% sharing and beats the round-8 insider in 36 of 50, so the direction holds more often than not; the sizes are not estimates.

    How to read it

    Each dot is a person, and each line a contact who can see what they post. In round 0 a few people see an accurate report. In every round, a person who is not yet sharing looks at their contacts. If the share of contacts they see sharing is larger than their private conviction Δ, the cost to them of repeating something they doubt, they start sharing too. They pass on the most alarming version they saw, and sometimes step it up a level.

    Fear changes both steps. A frightened person puts more weight on each contact who is sharing, and is more likely to embellish. A correction goes out once, to a random share of people. Sharers it reaches weigh it against what the claim means to them and how many of their contacts still share it. People it reaches before they share become harder to recruit.

    • The headline above the network shows the most common version in the current round, and the most alarming version still circulating if that differs. The bars show the full mix.
    • Rings on the network: a dashed ring marks someone the correction reached; a hollow dot with a solid green ring has retracted; a grey dot with a dashed green edge was reached before sharing.
    • With a correction on, the dashed line shows the same run, on the same random draws, with no correction. The gap is what the correction bought.
    • Hover a dot to see that person’s conviction and how many contacts are sharing.
    • The Cascade Game card is the nine-player example from the Moral Panic paper, reproduced exactly. It shows the same lock-in with no randomness at all.
    • “Keep this run to compare” draws the current run as a dotted line, so you can change one setting and see the difference.
    • The link in your address bar stores every setting and the random seed, so a run can be shared exactly.

    What the evidence says

    Published findings. These are measured, unlike the model above, which is a teaching device to be read against them.

    • False news travels further and faster. Vosoughi, Roy and Aral studied “~126,000 stories tweeted by ~3 million people more than 4.5 million times” on Twitter from 2006 to 2017. “Falsehood diffused significantly farther, faster, deeper, and more broadly than the truth in all categories of information.” In their research brief: “it took the truth approximately six times as long as falsehood to reach 1,500 people,” and “falsehoods were fully 70% more likely to be retweeted than the truth.” They also found that “false stories inspired fear, disgust, and surprise in replies.” Juul and Ugander later found that, once cascade size is controlled for, the structural differences between true and false news cascades “can almost entirely be explained by false-news cascades being larger,” that is, false news is more infectious rather than spreading by a different mechanism.
    • Thresholds decide collective outcomes. Granovetter defines a threshold as “the number or proportion of others who must make one decision before a given actor does so,” and warns that “Groups with similar average preferences may generate very different results.” Rumor diffusion is one of his suggested applications. Watts (2002) put fractional thresholds of this kind on a sparse random network, where large cascades become possible or impossible depending on connectivity and the spread of thresholds; this tool runs the same kind of rule on a small-world network. Moving the conviction spread slider shows this directly.
    • Corrections help but do not erase. A meta-analysis of 32 studies (N = 6,527) by Walter and Tukachinsky found that “on average, correction does not entirely eliminate the effect of misinformation (r = –.05, p = .045).” Corrections worked better when “delivered by the source of the misinformation itself” and were “less effective if … the misinformation has been repeated multiple times prior to correction, or when there was a time lag between the delivery of the misinformation and the correction.” The r is the average remaining effect of misinformation after correction.
    • Misinformation persists after debunking. Chan, Jones, Hall Jamieson and Albarracín (k = 52, N = 6,878) report effect sizes of d = 1.14–1.33 for debunking and d = 0.75–1.06 for “the persistence of misinformation in the face of debunking.” “Persistence was stronger and the debunking effect was weaker when audiences generated reasons in support of the initial misinformation.”
    • The messenger matters. Berinsky found that “Refuting rumors with statements from unlikely sources can, under certain circumstances, increase the willingness of citizens to reject rumors regardless of their own political predilections,” and that “merely repeating a rumor increases its power.”
    • Emotion raises belief in false news. Martel, Pennycook and Rand found “both correlational and causal evidence that reliance on emotion increases belief in fake news” (four experiments, total N = 3,884). The tool’s fear gain λ acts on sharing rather than belief; this study supports its direction, not its size.

    Method · the sharing rule

    The Mass Game (Walberg and co-author) pits an individual against the masses. θ is the share of the group endorsing the claim and does two jobs at once: it is the benefit of joining the wave and the ostracism cost of holding out. Δ is private conviction. If the masses share, the individual gets θ − Δ from sharing and −θ + Δ from holding out, so the best response is to share if and only if Δ < θ. In a population with convictions drawn from F, the share evolves as θt+1 = F(θt), and full conformity is absorbing.

    On a network each person sees only their own contacts. Person i shares when Δi < θ̂i, with θ̂i = min(1, λ·mi/ki + a), where mi of their ki contacts are sharing. The paper treats θ̂ − θ, the gap between perceived and true endorsement, as the lever a disinformer pulls; a is that lever. The fear gain λ comes from Walberg’s Narrative Warfare paper, where fear raises the weight a person puts on evidence and, on a network, “catching a neighbor’s emotion raises one’s own gain on all subsequent private signals.” Scaling the observed share by λ is this tool’s simplification of that proposition, not the paper’s own network model. Sharing is absorbing: people do not quietly stop, they only retract.

    Convictions are LogNormal with median Δ and shape σ (the paper’s numerical section uses shape 0.5), drawn once per seed so that moving a slider shifts every person together. A person who starts sharing passes on the most alarming version among their sharing contacts and steps it up one level with probability min(0.95, ε·λ).

    Method · the correction

    The correction follows Walberg’s Sticky Affect model. Belief is revised only if α(E)·evidence > (1 − α(E))·UI + C, where α(E) is the weight on accuracy, which falls as emotional activation E rises, UI is the identity value of the claim, and C = g(N, S) is the cost of abandoning it, rising in narrative coherence N and social reinforcement S. Here E = (λ − 1) + v/4 for a sharer holding version v, α(E) = 1/(1 + E), S is the share of the person’s contacts still sharing, and N grows with the rounds they have held the claim, saturating at 10. An in-group voice carries three times the evidence weight of an out-group fact-check, following the roughly 3:1 ratio in the Moral Panic paper’s falsification sweep. In that paper the weight shifts the perceived share θ̂; this tool applies the same ratio to the evidence term of the Sticky Affect condition, a simplification. People reached before they share add α(E)·evidence to their conviction. This is not prebunking in the inoculation sense: it is the same after-the-fact correction reaching someone who has not yet shared, and many of them have already seen the claim.

    What is notional

    Everything numeric in this tool is a teaching value, not an estimate. The outputs are properties of the model under these values, not findings about any real audience, platform or event.

    • Network: 160 people, each linked to their 3 nearest neighbours (about 3.6 contacts each after merging duplicates), 6% of links rewired at random.
    • Default median conviction 0.30, fear gain 1.4, amplified visibility 0.05, exaggeration 0.18 per retelling, four first witnesses.
    • Correction: evidence 0.6 (out-group) or 1.8 (in-group), UI = 0.25, C = 0.5·S + 0.5·N. The functional form α(E) = 1/(1 + E) satisfies the paper’s assumption that α falls in E; the paper does not fix a form.
    • The five-step claim ladder is illustrative wording written for this tool. It does not quote any real post or outlet.
    • The 3:1 in-group weight is itself a model output in the Moral Panic paper, not an empirical estimate.
    • Each run is one random draw of the network, convictions and correction reach (seed 7 by default). Other seeds give different numbers; “New network and people” draws another.

    Method · the Cascade Game

    Nine players with intensity pairs (xk, yk) from (2, 10) to (10, 2) choose Left or Right. Each earns yk per same-side partner on the Left and xk on the Right. From the paper’s starting split (players 1–5 Left), player 6 moves first because 7 × 3 = 21 < 5 × 5 = 25. Under “All who gain switch,” the card reproduces the paper’s four-round payoff table exactly. The paper defines a one-at-a-time rule; “Largest gain first” applies it and reaches the same unanimous end state in five rounds, with players 8 and 9 moving separately.

    Randomness comes from a seeded mulberry32 generator. The run with and without the correction share the same draws, so they are identical until the correction round.
