The threat that leaves something to chance

  1. The problem. Two sides want the same thing, and neither wants a disaster. A threat to start one on purpose is hard to believe, because carrying it out would hurt the side that made it too.
  2. Schelling's answer. Don't threaten to jump off the cliff; walk closer to the edge. Take steps that raise a risk of disaster that neither side fully controls. The threat is not that you will choose catastrophe, but that things might slip out of anyone's hands (Schelling 1960, ch. 8; 1966).
  3. A contest of nerve. A crisis then becomes a competition in risk-taking: who will put up with more shared danger before giving way? The side that cares more about the stake can stand more risk, so it tends to win (Powell 1988, 1990).
  4. Nobody knows the other's nerve. You don't know how much the Rival cares; it doesn't know how much you do. Every move is a signal, and each side updates its read of the other by Bayes' rule. Bluffs can work, and they can also end in disaster.

How a round works

  • You and the Rival choose at the same time: Raise the risk (adds 4 points to the shared risk, for good), Hold (keep the pressure; the risk still creeps up 1 point), or Back down (concede the stake).
  • If one side backs down, the other wins the stake. If both back down together, it is split.
  • If neither backs down, a die is rolled against the risk meter. Under it: disaster, and both lose 100 points. Otherwise, next round. After six rounds an unresolved crisis is split.
  • Five crises, each with its own stake. Your resolve and the Rival's are drawn fresh each time, so you must read the Rival again.

      How the game works, and the theory behind it
      • Payoffs. Disaster costs each side 100. Winning a stake is worth the crisis's stake times your resolve: ×0.4 for low, ×1 for medium, ×2.5 for high. Backing down is worth 0; a split is worth half. Your score is the sum over five crises.
      • Risk. The meter starts at 2%. Each Raise adds 4 points for good; every round both sides stay in adds 1 more. Nobody fully controls it, which is the point: the danger is shared.
      • Resolve. Each side's resolve is drawn from the crisis's known odds (shown as the starting belief bars). Only you see yours.
      • The Rival's strategy. It uses a cutoff: it backs down once the risk passes a level that rises with how much it values the stake, and shifts with what it believes about you (it hangs on longer if you look weak, gives way sooner if you look resolved). Before each move it looks ahead, imagining many ways the crisis could unfold if both sides then follow that cutoff rule, and picks the move with the best average, with a little noise.
      • Reading each other. Each side reads the other's moves by Bayes' rule, assuming the other follows the cutoff rule and allowing that any move might be a slip. A Raise says "I can afford more risk", so it moves the read toward high resolve.
      • Equilibrium play. The overlay and the benchmark use the same strategy as the Rival, in your seat. It is equilibrium-inspired, not a solved equilibrium: the cutoff rule is a simple approximation and the look-ahead is one step of improvement on it.

      In 1,000 test series against the Rival: playing the computer's resolve-aware strategy averaged 103 points; random moves 80; always raising 73; a cutoff that ignores your own resolve 71; always backing down 69; always holding 20. Always raising or always holding wins most crises, but 26–30% of crises then end in disaster.
