Fictional and notional. Two invented sides, Red and Blue. Every number is illustrative game design, not data or a prediction.

Pick a side

Red runs a big exercise twice a quarter, on a published calendar. This time it may be cover for a real attack. Over twelve weeks Red builds readiness, or only trains; Blue watches through four kinds of collection, each blind to something, and has to decide whether, and when, to warn. Warn too late and the attack lands. Warn at an exercise and Blue loses credibility and shows Red which sources it was relying on.

    How the game works
    • Red has 6 activity points a week. Three activities build readiness: stockpiling logistics, moving reserves and readying key units (each point counts for less after the first in the same week). Exercise tempo builds none but is expected inside the published windows. Deception hides part of this week’s preparation; communications discipline hides it from signals. Preparation can be hidden inside a scheduled exercise or a declared snap exercise, at a cost in speed. From readiness 50 Red may attack; the odds rise with readiness and fall as Blue mobilizes.
    • Blue has 6 collection points a week across imagery, signals, human and open sources. Each source reads some Red activities well and others poorly, and more points mean less noise. Blue sees only the readings, and what is public: the calendar and any snap exercise.
    • Warning starts mobilization, which grows every week and cuts Red’s odds. Standing a warning down costs credibility (and slower mobilization next time) and shows Red which sources Blue was leaning on, which then read less.
    • Winning. Red wins if its attack succeeds, or, if it only exercised, if Blue warned. Blue wins if the attack fails or never comes, or if it kept quiet through an exercise. Scores (0–100) also count mobilization, credibility and wasted weeks.
    • The computer. Computer Red draws an intent and a deception style and adapts to how suspicious it thinks Blue is. Computer Blue is a Bayesian: it spends collection where it best separates “attack” from “exercise” and warns on its posterior.

    All numbers are illustrative game design. In 1,000 computer-against-computer games, Red won 42.6% and Blue 57.4%. Against a Red that meant to attack, Blue stopped or deterred the attack 47% of the time; against an exercise-only Red that ran feints, Blue cried wolf 36% of the time. A Blue that never warns scores 62 on average, one that warns in week 1 scores 57, and the computer Blue 67.

Betts, Surprise Attack: Lessons for Defense Planning (Brookings Institution, 1982). The game’s mechanics are original and do not model any case in these books.
