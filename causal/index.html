Causal Inference, Plainly

Start here

Every "X caused Y" claim compares what happened with a world you never see.

The short versionTo say a US transit caused more PLA flights, you need to know how many flights there would have been without the transit, on the same days. That second number never happens, so you can't observe it. Causal inference is the toolkit for making a believable guess about it.

The picture to keep in your head made-up data

What happened (the transit took place)What would have happened without it

The solid line is in your data. The dashed line never is. The effect is the gap between them. Every method in this course is a different way of drawing the dashed line, and each one works only under its own condition. Your job in a paper is to say which method you used and why its condition is believable in your case.

How each page works

Each page asks one question and answers it in two sentences. Then you get one thing to play with. The data in the tools are simulated, so the true effect is always known, and you can watch a method hit it or miss it. Jargon is folded away under "The technical terms" at the bottom of each page. Use the Next button or your arrow keys.

Part 1 · The basic problem

What does "X caused Y" actually mean?

The short answerEvery case has two possible results: one with X and one without. The effect is the difference between them. You only ever get to see one of the two.

Everyday version. You take an aspirin and your headache goes away. Did the aspirin do it? You'd have to know whether the headache would have gone away anyway. That's the same afternoon, without the aspirin, and you can't rerun it.

Eight days, two possible results each made-up numbers

On each day the US either sent a ship through the Strait or didn't. The table shows PLA aircraft counts. "?" is the result that didn't happen. Press the button to see it.

In your work

In Calibrated Coercion, the claim "transits raise PLA activity" means activity on transit days was higher than it would have been on those same days without a transit. Every design choice in the paper is about how you fill in that missing column.

The technical terms
Potential outcomes
The two results, written Y(1) with treatment and Y(0) without. The effect for one case is Y(1) − Y(0).
The fundamental problem of causal inference
Holland's (1986) name for the fact that you only ever see one of the two.
ATE, ATT
The average effect across all cases (ATE), or across only the cases that got X (ATT). Saying which one you want before you estimate anything is called "choosing your estimand."

Part 1 · The basic problem

Why can't I just compare days with and without a transit?

The short answerBecause those days were different to begin with. If the Navy sails when things are already tense, transit days would have had more PLA flights anyway. The simple comparison mixes that head start in with the real effect.

Everyday version. People in hospitals are sicker than people at home. That doesn't mean hospitals make you sick. The people who went in were sicker before they arrived.

Transits and PLA flights over 2,000 simulated days true effect is about 9–10 aircraft

Slide to the right to make the Navy more likely to sail on tense days. Watch the simple comparison pull away from the truth.

Not at all (like a coin flip)Strongly

In your work

This is the first thing a referee will raise about Calibrated Coercion: "Doesn't the US schedule transits after provocations?" If it does, part of what looks like a PLA response was already on its way.

The technical terms
Selection bias
The "head start" bar: how different the treated cases would have been even without treatment. The simple comparison = true effect on the treated + selection bias.
Confounder
The thing that causes both (here, tension). It drives the Navy's choice and PLA activity.
Identification
An argument for why your comparison has no head start, or for how you removed it. Every method from here on is an identification strategy.

Part 1 · The basic problem

Why do experiments work?

The short answerA coin flip doesn't know which days are tense, so tense and calm days end up spread evenly across both groups. With no head start, the simple comparison is now an honest estimate.

40 days, placed by how tense they were true effect = 9

Each dot is a day, placed by how tense it was. Compare "Navy picks" with "Coin flip," then flip a few more times.

Notice that a single coin flip is never perfect. The estimate bounces around 9. The size of that bounce is what a standard error measures. More cases make it smaller.

In your work

Sonic Vectors is the one project where you control the coin. Its main risk is subjects talking to each other: if a treated subject tells a control subject about the audio, the control group is no longer clean.

The technical terms
Randomization
Assigning treatment by chance. It removes selection bias on average, for things you measured and things you didn't.
Balance
The two groups look alike before treatment. The dots above show balance on tension.
Power
The chance your study detects an effect of a given size if it's real. Small samples have low power.
SUTVA / interference
The assumption that one case's treatment doesn't affect another case's outcome. Spillovers between subjects, or between countries, break it.

Part 2 · When you can't run an experiment

What should I control for, and what shouldn't I?

The short answerControl for things that cause both X and Y. Don't control for things that X itself causes. That either erases part of the effect or creates a fake one.

Rule of thumb. Ask of every control: "Was this already set before X happened?" If yes, it's usually safe. If X could have changed it, leave it out.

Pick your controls true total effect = 9

In your work

In Calibrated Coercion, control for pre-transit tension. Don't control for anything measured after the ship shows up, like alert levels or the volume of PRC statements that day. Those are part of the response you're trying to measure.

The technical terms
DAG
Directed acyclic graph: the arrow diagram above. You draw your assumptions, and the graph tells you which controls are needed.
Confounder, mediator, collider
Tension is a confounder (control for it). Alert level is a mediator (controlling for it gives only the "direct" effect). News coverage is a collider (controlling for it creates bias). Mediators and colliders together are called "bad controls."
Backdoor criterion
The formal rule: control for enough to block every non-causal path from X to Y, and nothing that X causes.

Part 2 · When you can't run an experiment

What about the things I can't measure?

The short answerIf something you didn't measure pushes both X and Y, your estimate is off, and no number of other controls will fix it. What you can do is say how strong that hidden factor would have to be to overturn your result.

NFL spending and wins, with a hidden factor true effect = 3

The hidden factor is front-office skill. Good front offices spend differently and win more. Make it matter more and watch the estimate drift.

Not at allA lot

How fragile is your result? works on any regression

Enter the t-statistic on your main variable and your sample size.

In your work

For the NFL roster paper, the obvious objection is "good GMs build rosters well and win." Report the fragility number and compare it with a factor you did measure, like payroll. Then a reader can judge whether a hidden factor that strong is plausible.

The technical terms
Omitted variable bias (OVB)
The error from leaving out a confounder. Its size = (how much the hidden factor affects Y) × (how strongly it moves with X).
Robustness value
Cinelli & Hazlett (2020). The share of leftover variation in both X and Y that a hidden factor would need to explain to wipe the estimate out. Computed from t and degrees of freedom. The R package is sensemakr.
Benchmarking
Comparing that number with the strength of a control you do have, so the threshold means something.

Part 2 · When you can't run an experiment

How do I compare like with like?

The short answerFor each case that got X, find a case without X that looks the same on what matters. Then compare the pairs. This fixes differences you measured. It can't fix ones you didn't.

Everyday version. To test whether tutoring helps, don't compare tutored students with everyone. Compare each tutored student with an untutored one who had the same grades last year.

NHL no-trade clauses and trades, 1,200 simulated players clauses do nothing here: true effect = 0

Better players are more likely to get clauses, and better players are traded less anyway. Make that link stronger.

No differenceMuch more likely

In your work

The NHL trade protection paper matches on player quality. A referee will ask "who gets clauses?" If agents or teams know something about the player that isn't in your data, matching can't remove it. That's why the cutoff design two pages ahead is stronger.

The technical terms
Selection on observables
The assumption that once you account for measured traits, who got X is as good as random. Also called "no unmeasured confounding" or "conditional ignorability."
Overlap
Every treated case needs comparable untreated cases. If the best players all have clauses, there's no one to match them to.
Propensity score
The predicted chance of getting X, given measured traits. You can match on that one number instead of every trait.

Part 3 · Natural experiments

What if something random nudges X?

The short answerFind something that pushes X around for reasons unrelated to Y and that affects Y only through X. Then use only the part of X it moves. That part is as good as randomly assigned.

Two rules. (1) The nudge has to really move X. A weak nudge gives wild answers. (2) The nudge can't affect Y any other way. This second rule can't be tested with data. You have to argue it.

Weather, PLA sorties and a risk index, 100 simulated studies true effect = 0.5

Bad weather grounds PLA aircraft for reasons that have nothing to do with politics. Try the three cases.

In your work

The Weather & PLA paper lives or dies on rule 2. Mellon (AJPS 2025) lists dozens of ways weather affects outcomes directly. Expect a referee to ask whether planners schedule around forecasts. Using the surprise part of the weather (realized minus forecast) is the cleaner nudge.

The technical terms
Instrumental variable (IV)
The nudge (here, weather). X is the "endogenous" variable, and the IV estimate is the nudge's effect on Y divided by its effect on X.
Relevance, exclusion
Rule 1 and rule 2. Relevance is checked with the first-stage F-statistic (weak if roughly under 10). Exclusion is argued, not tested.
LATE
IV estimates the effect for cases the nudge actually moves, which may not be everyone.

Part 3 · Natural experiments

What if a rule draws a line?

The short answerWhen a rule treats cases just above a cutoff differently from cases just below, those near the line are almost identical. So the jump in Y right at the line is the effect.

Everyday version. Students who score 80 get a scholarship and students who score 79 don't. A 79 and an 80 are basically the same student, so comparing them isolates the scholarship.

Trade probability by seasons from the cutoff true jump = −8 points

Narrow the window to compare only players near the line. Then let players game the cutoff.

Very close (fewer players, noisier)Everyone

In your work

For the NHL paper, the cutoff is Group 3 free agency (7 accrued seasons or age 27). A no-trade clause can be signed earlier, but it can only take effect once the player reaches that status. Because not everyone at the line gets a clause, you'd scale the jump by how much clause rates jump.

The technical terms
Regression discontinuity (RD)
The design above. The variable that determines the cutoff (seasons) is the "running variable."
Bandwidth
The window around the line. Narrower means less bias and more noise. Software (rdrobust) picks it for you.
Fuzzy RD
When crossing the line raises the chance of treatment rather than guaranteeing it. Divide the jump in Y by the jump in treatment.
Manipulation test
Check for a pile-up of cases just past the cutoff. A pile-up suggests gaming.

Part 3 · Natural experiments

Can I compare before and after?

The short answerNot on its own, because lots of things change over time. Compare how much the treated group changed with how much a similar untreated group changed over the same period. That works if the two groups would have moved in parallel without the event.

A treated group and a comparison group, 24 months true effect = 6

The dashed line is where the treated group would have gone without the event. Make the treated group already be on a steeper path and see what happens.

Parallel pathsMuch steeper
Treated groupComparison groupTreated, without the event

The check is to look left of the event line. If the two groups weren't moving together before the event, there's no reason to think they would have afterwards.

In your work

Calibrated Coercion and Simple Simon both use this logic over short windows. In Simple Simon, the comparison is small trades in the same market and hour, which stand in for how the price would have moved anyway.

The technical terms
Difference-in-differences (DiD)
(Treated after − treated before) − (comparison after − comparison before).
Parallel trends
The assumption that the dashed line runs parallel to the comparison group.
Event study
The same idea, plotted month by month. The months before the event ("leads") should sit near zero.
Staggered adoption
When cases get treated at different times, the standard regression can give wrong, even wrong-signed, answers. Use Callaway & Sant'Anna (2021) or similar estimators.

Part 3 · Natural experiments

What if no single comparison case fits?

The short answerBuild a blend of untreated cases that tracks the treated case closely before the event. After the event, the blend is your best guess at the dashed line.

One treated case, three possible comparison cases true effect = +5

Mix cases A and B (C gets whatever is left). A good blend hugs the treated line before month 13. Or let the computer find it.

Treated caseThe blendComparison cases

In your work

Useful when there's one treated unit, like Taiwan after a specific policy change, and you need a stand-in built from other countries or theaters.

The technical terms
Synthetic control
Abadie and co-authors. Weights are non-negative and sum to one, so the blend stays within the range of real cases.
Pre-period fit
How closely the blend tracks the treated case before the event. A poor fit means a poor stand-in.
Placebo test
Pretend each comparison case was treated and see whether the real gap stands out from those fake ones.

Part 4 · Traps common in IR

If X comes first and predicts Y, doesn't that prove X causes Y?

The short answerNo. If one decision drives both X and Y and shows up in X a little earlier, X will predict Y every time, even if X does nothing.

Everyday version. Christmas ads appear before Christmas shopping peaks. The ads help a bit, but mostly both are driven by the same calendar.

PRC rhetoric and PLA action, 600 simulated weeks rhetoric does nothing here: true effect = 0

A hidden decision in Beijing drives both. Change how much later it shows up in action than in rhetoric.

In your work

This is the core worry in Talk and Tempo. Rhetoric may be an early sign of a decision already made, not a cause of what follows. Calling it a "leading indicator" is a claim the data can support. Calling it a "cause" needs a design that breaks the common driver.

The technical terms
Granger causality
X "Granger-causes" Y if past X helps predict Y beyond Y's own past. It measures predictive order, not causation.
Common shock
The hidden decision in the tool. Whether a test catches it depends on how many lags you include.

Part 4 · Traps common in IR

What if the actor chooses X because of what it knows and you don't?

The short answerStates reveal weapons, send ships and issue threats when they expect it to work. So the cases you see were picked using private knowledge. Comparing "revealed" with "concealed" mixes the effect of revealing with the reasons for revealing.

Revealing a weapon and winning a dispute, 3,000 simulated disputes revealing does nothing here: true effect = 0

Strong states reveal and also win more often. You can only control for an outside guess at strength. Improve that guess.

Rough guessPerfect

You'll never measure a state's private information perfectly. That's why work like Secret Weapons writes down a game model of the choice and estimates it, instead of only adding controls.

In your work

In Secret Weapons / SitG, the game model is the identification strategy. The referee's question shifts from "what did you control for?" to "is the game right, and does it fit data it wasn't built on?"

The technical terms
Strategic selection
Signorino's term: who gets treated is an equilibrium choice. Regression on the observed cases is biased, often badly.
Structural estimation
Estimating the parameters of the game itself. For QRE models, use NPL or constrained MLE (Crisman-Cox & Gibilisco).

Part 4 · Traps common in IR

What if the way the data are counted changes?

The short answerA change in how something is counted looks exactly like a change in the thing itself. Before reading any jump as an effect, check whether the source changed what it reports.

PLA aircraft reported vs. real, 24 months real activity doesn't change

Before month 15 the source misses some activity. Then it starts counting everything. Make the earlier undercount bigger.

NothingMost
ReportedReal

In your work

Taiwan's MND reports activity across three channels, and every month before July 2026 is undercounted. MASTER_ADIZ also has about 52 weeks of false zeros from February 2024 to January 2025. Any before/after comparison that crosses those dates will pick this up as a fake effect.

The technical terms
Differential measurement error
Error that differs between periods or groups. It creates fake effects.
Attenuation
Random noise in X shrinks the estimate toward zero. That's a bias too, in the opposite direction.
Construct validity
Whether your measure tracks the concept at all. Talk and Tempo's construct check is this question.

Part 4 · Traps common in IR

What does a null result actually tell me?

The short answer"Not significant" doesn't mean "no effect." It means you can't rule out zero. The useful question is the reverse one: how big an effect can you rule out?

Your estimate and its uncertainty

Set an estimate and its standard error. The bar is the 95% range of effects your data are consistent with.

Testing many things at once

In your work

Election Cycle runs 28 series, so a few "hits" are expected by luck. A placebo answers that only if it's applied to one overall number, such as the count of significant series. Exercises as Theater and Talk and Tempo report nulls. Say which effect sizes the data rule out, not only that the estimate wasn't significant.

The technical terms
Minimum detectable effect (MDE)
About 2.8 × the standard error for 80% power at the 5% level.
Equivalence test (TOST)
Tests whether the effect is smaller than a bound you name in advance. That's how a "bounded null" is shown formally.
Multiple testing
With many tests, false positives pile up. Fixes include pre-registration, adjusted p-values and joint tests.

Glossary

Counterfactual
What would have happened without X. The dashed line.
Estimand
The exact effect you're after (for example, the average effect on transit days).
Selection bias
The head start treated cases had before treatment.
Confounder
Something that causes both X and Y.
Identification
Your argument for why the comparison has no head start.
Bad control
A control that X itself affects.
Robustness value
How strong a hidden factor would need to be to erase your result.
Instrument
A nudge that moves X and touches Y only through X.
Regression discontinuity
Comparing cases just either side of a cutoff.
Parallel trends
Treated and comparison groups would have moved together without the event.
Standard error
How much the estimate would bounce around if you reran the study.
Power
The chance of detecting a real effect of a given size.
