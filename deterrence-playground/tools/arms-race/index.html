How to read it

Lewis Fry Richardson, a Quaker physicist and meteorologist, wrote an arms race as two equations. Each side's arms grow in response to the other side's arms, shrink under the cost of what it already holds, and carry a constant push that Richardson called grievance:

dx/dt = k·y − a·x + g
dy/dt = l·x − b·y + h

Here k and l are the defence (reaction) coefficients, a and b measure fatigue and expense, and g and h are the grievances. Negative grievances pull arms down; the tool calls them goodwill. The phase plane plots A's arms across and B's arms up. The arrows show where each pair of levels is heading. The two straight lines mark where one side stops changing, and the race comes to rest where they cross.

Everything turns on one comparison. If fatigue outweighs reaction, ab > kl, every race settles at the same balance, wherever it starts. If reaction outweighs fatigue, ab < kl, the balance is unstable: races run away, or, with enough goodwill, collapse toward disarmament, depending on where they start. Method

Two sides. The equations, the names of the coefficients and the stability discussion follow Ron Smith's 2019 review of the model, which writes α and β for a and b. The equilibrium is x* = (bg + kh)/(ab − kl), y* = (ah + lg)/(ab − kl). It is stable when both eigenvalues of the matrix [[−a, k], [l, −b]] are negative, which for non-negative coefficients means ab > kl. Paths are integrated with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta step of 0.1 years. In a saddle the dashed line through the equilibrium separates paths that run away from paths that disarm.

Three sides, an extension. Each country's arms respond to both rivals: dxi/dt = Σj kijxj − aixi + gi. The trio is stable when all three eigenvalues of the 3 × 3 system matrix have negative real parts. The generalization is standard linear algebra; the U.S.–Russia–China setting and every coefficient are this tool's. We did not find a published three-party Richardson model of these three countries that we could open and check, so none is cited. Smith (2019) notes the general result the tool illustrates: pairs can be stable while the triplet is not.

Real data. The overlay plots the FAS stockpile estimates for the United States (x) and the USSR, then Russia (y), in thousands of warheads, from Our World in Data. The fit regresses each side's change from one year to the next on the rival's stockpile, its own stockpile and a constant, over the years you choose, and draws the path those fitted coefficients produce from the first year of the window. This fit is illustrative. Richardson modelled defence spending, not warhead counts; stockpiles also moved with arms control treaties, retirements and technology that the equations do not contain; and annual differences only approximate the continuous equations. Fitted coefficients with the wrong sign, such as a negative reaction, are shown as they come out.

What is notional: every coefficient in the presets and the three-party model. What is real: the warhead series and the starting stockpiles in the three-party chart.
